Curved Market Space
For Luca Gaspari · run 23 October 2026

Curved market space, tested

Luca, you sent two ideas. We wrote both down as testable claims, fixed every rule before looking at a single result, and ran them on twenty years of US stock prices. This page shows what happened, how each test was built, and where the tests and your ideas part ways.

Your two ideas

A

Macro beats micro

Computing every stock's exact state is a three-body problem, and impossible. That doesn't stop a good approximation of the whole. An aggregate (the market, a fund, a group of related stocks) should be more forecastable than the stocks in it, the way temperature is forecastable and molecules aren't.

B

The market's space is curved, and the curvature matters

Treat correlations as a metric, the way general relativity treats the metric tensor. The curvature of that space should carry information about where aggregates go next. It should warn of trouble and improve forecasts.

Paraphrased from your two messages of 23 October, as the test plan records them. B is A's tool, so every geometry test below forecasts the market, funds or groups, never single stocks.

The short answer

Neither idea held up in this test. Baskets of stocks, all the way up to the whole market, were no more forecastable in direction than single stocks, and the curvature of the market's correlation graph added nothing to forecasts of its next-month direction or risk once the plain counting statistics it is mostly made of were in the model.

These four primary conclusions are judged together under Holm's correction. For any of them to pass, the smallest one-sided p, 0.382, would have had to be below 0.0125 (0.05 ÷ 4). None passes. Each also had to clear the project's own bar for a new idea, t ≥ 3, and none came close.

The one money rule, holding 100% SPY when curvature sat on its calm side and 50% otherwise, matched random timing (−0.3 points a year, p 0.61) and trailed SPY by 3.05 points a year after costs.

What held up

The thermodynamics half of your analogy. Risk is forecastable at every level of aggregation: the volatility forecaster beat its historical mean for single stocks, baskets and the market (market Clark-West t 3.17 at 5 days, 3.29 at 21), and the plain yardsticks forecast SPY's next-month volatility with an out-of-sample R² of +38.2%. Curvature didn't add to that.

What this can't settle

The aggregation test turned out weak: even the volatility control, which is forecastable everywhere, failed its slope test, so a small aggregation effect could hide in A. And no test here rules out other forecasters, better data (these stocks are survivors only) or a law nobody has written down yet.

How the ideas were tested

The rules went into a written plan before any result existed: the stocks, the dates, the forecasters, the pass bars and the sign that counts as "crisis". Each test gets its own verdict. Scoring runs on 2008–2019 (2006–2019 for the covariance test). Later months are reported, and don't decide anything.

Three geometries, not one

The word "geometry" covers three separate constructions here. Their distances, curvatures and geodesics are different objects, and a result in one says nothing about another. General relativity supplies the vocabulary, not a theory linking them.

G

The stock graph

Nodes are stocks. Each month, edges join the 5% most-correlated pairs among the 500 most-traded US stocks over the last 63 trading days: 6,237 edges, each of length d = √(2(1 − ρ)). Curvature means the discrete Ricci curvatures of that graph, Ollivier's and Forman's.

S

The macro state space

Points are (market return, volatility, average correlation), with a metric learned from how the state moves. Do markets follow its geodesics? The most literal reading of "space tells matter how to move".

IDEA A

Is the whole more forecastable than its parts?

The prior going in: markets aren't low-dimensional chaos. They are hard to forecast mainly because news arrives at random and traders remove any pattern they can see. Averaging stocks cancels each company's own news, but not market-wide news, and market-wide news is what an aggregate's direction is made of. So the plan expected A to hold for risk and fail for direction.

Each month from 2003, the 500 most-traded stocks were rebuilt into a ladder: single stocks; 1,000 random equal-weight baskets each of 5, 25 and 100 stocks; and the equal-weight market of about 1,290 stocks. The same five forecasters ran on every rung.

Skill is out-of-sample R² against each level's own historical mean. Measured against zero instead, a rising market looks forecastable when it isn't: at 21 days SPY scores +2.7% to +4.2% against zero, but −1.5% to +0.1% against its own average. That gap is drift.

A1 fails, and the test had little power

24 of the 25 return R²s are at or below zero. The only positive one is the last-month return at 21 days on the market, +0.55%, with a 90% interval from −2.73 to +3.55. Its slope, +0.078 R² points per unit of ln size (interval −0.38 to +0.49), is the best of the five, at p 0.382 against a bar of 0.0013. The market's best Clark-West t is 0.71, against 3. For four forecasters, R² falls from single stocks to 100-stock baskets.

The control failed its own slope test. Volatility is forecastable on every rung, from +4.1% at single stocks to +7.4% at 100-stock baskets, and the market's Clark-West t is 3.17. Yet its slope p is 0.25 (0.35 at 21 days), because the market rung, at +6.6%, sits below the 100-baskets. Three things weaken the slope: the market rung is one series of 144 months; the fitting penalty grows up the ladder (the sign-flip null's slope is centred at −0.06 to −0.18, not 0); and the bar is t ≥ 3. So a small real aggregation effect in direction could hide here. What the run does show is that direction R² is negative on every rung, not small and positive.

2010–2014 is the only stretch that leaned your way: the market's last-month-return R² was +3.3% and the slope +0.48 (p 0.05). It didn't carry over to 2015–2019, 2020–2025 or the unseen months.

A2 · Do co-moving groups beat look-alike baskets?

Groups are the universe stocks that share a twin fund, about 32 a month with a median of 9 stocks. About 76% of group-months have a sector or industry fund as the twin, so the "structure" here is mostly industry. Each group is compared with random baskets matched member by member on market beta and volatility (20 replicates, each with its own fit).

Groups lose less than matched baskets for all five forecasters, but both are below zero. The best gap, trend at 21 days, +1.31 points, has p 0.017, or 0.084 after Holm across the five, so A2 is not passed, and the full family of secondaries can only raise that. IDEA B · GEOMETRY G

The curvature of the stock graph

On a Riemannian manifold, positive Ricci curvature makes nearby geodesics converge, as on a sphere, and negative curvature makes them spread, as on a saddle. Ollivier's coarse Ricci curvature asks the same question of a graph. Put half a unit of mass on stock x and spread the other half evenly over its neighbours; do the same for y. If moving one blob onto the other costs less than the distance between x and y, the neighbourhoods are closer than their centres, and κ = 1 − W₁ / d is positive. On a smooth manifold with small neighbourhoods it recovers the Ricci curvature along x→y, up to scale.

Forman's curvature is combinatorial: F = 4 − deg x − deg y + 3t, with t the triangles on the edge. Averaged over a graph with m edges and T triangles it collapses to F̄ = 4 − Σd²/m + 9T/m, a function of degree concentration and triangle count alone. Try both below.

B1 · What is curvature made of?

Four plain graph statistics explain 92% of the Ollivier series and 100% of Forman's.

Across 270 months, a regression of each curvature series on degree concentration (Σd²/m), triangles per edge (T/m), the edges' mean correlation and the largest connected component's share reproduces 92% of the variance of mean Ollivier curvature (94% over 2008–2019, 93% on the 500-stock months alone). Forman is an exact function of the same statistics at a fixed edge count, so its R² is 1.000. The market yardsticks (average correlation, the absorption ratio, SPY's 63-day volatility) explain much less: 25% of Ollivier, 39% of Forman. Whether the remainder is useful is B2's question, and the answer is no.

Does curvature warn of trouble?

Ollivier curvature rises in crises (Sandhu, Georgiou & Tannenbaum 2016; Samal et al. 2021 cite the same), and Forman turns more negative. Sandhu et al. call the high-curvature state "robust"; here the crisis side is simply the side crash months sit on, read from the papers before any test. The question is whether it moves before the fall or with it.

B2 · Does curvature add to forecasts?

This is the closest test of your geometric claim. Each month, four nested forecasts of SPY's next 21 trading days were fitted on all earlier months by least squares:

  • Model 1, the baseline: SPY's 21- and 63-day volatility, average correlation and the absorption ratio.
  • Model 2: the baseline plus the four plain graph statistics.
  • Model 3: model 2 plus mean Ollivier curvature.
  • Model 4: the baseline plus mean Forman curvature, which can't add anything beyond model 2.

The key comparison is model 3 against model 2, scored over 2008–2019 by Clark-West with a bar of t ≥ 3, for direction (SPY's excess return) and for risk (its log realized volatility).

The published-style graph is the one the papers use: the minimum spanning tree plus every pair with ρ ≥ 0.75 (Samal et al.'s main value), on the same 63-day window. Samal et al.'s Forman is their weighted form, with the edge weight guessed as the distance.

Neither target passes: direction t +0.21 (p 0.417), risk t −0.87 (p 0.807). No model forecasts direction at all. Against the historical mean of excess returns, the out-of-sample R² over 2008–2019 is −8.8% for the baseline and −17.2% for model 3; every added input made it worse. Risk is forecastable with or without curvature: +38.2% for the baseline, +36.9% for model 3.

One comparison came near. Forman against the baseline, for risk, reached t 2.74 over 2008–2019, below the bar of 3. It reversed in 2020–2025 (t −0.75), and model 2, which holds the very statistics Forman is made of, didn't help (t 0.89), so the plan reads it as "graph statistics help", not "curvature helps". The comparisons outside the key row are reported uncorrected, one of 7 per target and period.

B3 · Money tests

The rule held 100% SPY when Ollivier curvature was below its trailing median (the calm side) and 50% otherwise, decided after each month-end close and traded at the next close, with cash earning the T-bill. Over 242 months, 2005-04 to 2025-06, it switched 61 times.

It returned +8.0% a year before costs against the random timings' median +8.3%, so it trails random by 0.3 points a year (p 0.61), and after costs it trailed SPY by 3.05 points a year (3.40 with cash at 0). It lost less in its worst drawdown, 31% against SPY's 55%, but the plain volatility threshold also lost 31%, and volatility-managed SPY at the same exposure lost 22.5%. Against the three baselines it had to beat over 2005-04 to 2019-12 (a constant 74% allocation, the volatility threshold, and the better of two earlier correlation rules), its mean monthly net differences were −0.083%, −0.079% and −0.100% (t −1.04, −1.22, −1.70).

Curvature made the fund portfolio riskier

The second money test is risk management only. A long-only portfolio of the twin funds (a median of 12 a month, 2010-08 to 2019-12) is weighted by 1 ÷ forecast volatility, with three forecasts that differ only in whether the group's curvature is in them. The pass needed the curvature version to be calmer. It was riskier: the paired monthly variance difference, without minus with, is −7.3 × 10⁻⁶, t −3.84. The size is tiny, 0.03 points of annual volatility, and plain trailing volatility did best. In 2020–2025 it went the same way (t −2.17).

IDEA B · GEOMETRY M

B5 · The covariance manifold

Covariance matrices live on the cone of positive-definite matrices. B5 asks whether averaging them in matrix-log coordinates, exp(w₁ log S₂₁ + w₂ log S₆₃ + w₃ log S₂₅₂), forecasts the next 21 days' covariance better than averaging them as plain matrices, w₁S₂₁ + w₂S₆₃ + w₃S₂₅₂. The two share the windows, the shrinkage, the 66-point weight grid and the fitting; only the representation differs. Each is judged by the realized risk of the capped minimum-variance portfolio it builds.

The log-Euclidean metric is the Euclidean metric pulled back through the matrix logarithm, so it is flat. Even a pass would have supported a representation, not market curvature.

Bars share one scale from 0 to 18% a year. The affine step is reported only.

They built almost the same portfolio, 12.37% against 12.38% a year over 2006–2019 (t −0.15), because both chose mostly the 252-day matrix: the Euclidean combination put weight 0.9 on it in 126 of 167 months, the log-Euclidean 0.8 in 133. Log-Euclidean had the lower risk in 50% of months.

The curved metric got one reported test: one step along the affine-invariant geodesic past the latest covariance, BA⁻¹B. It is a valid covariance every month (its smallest eigenvalue stays positive), but its condition number reached 1.6 × 10⁹, and its portfolio was riskier than the existing estimator's, 13.0% against 12.4% (t −3.32). Stepping along the geodesic past the newest matrix extrapolates estimation noise. It is valid mathematics, and statistically unstable.

Where the analogy breaks

  • No field equation. In general relativity, matter determines the metric by a law. Here the metric is estimated from noisy data, and no law says how money bends it. Measuring curvature isn't the same as having a theory of it.
  • The metric is mostly estimation. A 500 × 500 covariance from 63 days is singular, with about 125,000 numbers estimated from about 31,500 returns. Shrinkage makes it usable, and noise has curvature too: Ollivier curvature computed from a window's odd days and from its even days correlates only 0.53 across months.
  • Delays are bounded only at daily resolution. Changes in gravity travel at a finite speed. The market analogue, a move in one stock that reaches another later, was looked for in an earlier census of lagged correlations in this project, which on daily closes found them at about the rate chance produces. That bounds such delays at that resolution. It doesn't rule out ones too weak to detect on single pairs, faster than a day, or that changed sign between periods.
  • Planets don't read forecasts. Traders do, and a curvature that predicted returns would be traded until it stopped.
  • There's no time in this metric. Each month's graph is a snapshot: a positive-definite geometry on stocks rather than a spacetime. Time enters only as the sequence of snapshots.

What these results can and can't show

  • A1 asked whether time-varying predictability rises with aggregation, against the historical mean. Not shown: against the historical mean no level was forecastable by these signals, so there was nothing for aggregation to raise.
  • It can't show that aggregates are unforecastable, only that five plain price forecasters, each calibrated by one straight line per level, didn't forecast them. Many-characteristic forecasts like those of Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020) weren't tested.
  • The ladder had little power, and the failed control shows it. A small real effect could hide; a large one wouldn't have produced negative R² everywhere.
  • A2: groups lose less than matched baskets, but both are below zero, and most of the gap appears with no real relation at all.
  • B2, the closest test of the geometric claim, failed for direction and for risk. Curvature here is mostly simpler statistics renamed, and the part ordinary factors don't produce is slightly negative.
  • B3 is one allocation rule; it matched random timing at the same exposure. B5 is about representing covariances, not about market curvature.
  • No result here could show that relativity's equations, or any law like them, govern markets.

Caveats

  • Survivor-only data. The stocks are those still listed, so firms that failed, such as Lehman Brothers in 2008, are missing. That can change the correlation structure in exactly the crises where curvature is meant to matter. Every crisis-period result is flagged as possibly biased; 2008–2009 was the worst stretch for every A forecaster and the largest A2 gap.
  • Few independent events. Twenty years hold only five SPY drawdowns of 15% or more.
  • 2020–2025 is not a holdout. Nearly every earlier experiment in the project read it, and the yardsticks used here were chosen knowing how they did in it. It is reported as a chronological evaluation period.
  • Reported comparisons are uncorrected. A few outside the key tests looked better in 2020–2025 after doing nothing in 2008–2019: the published-style graph's Ollivier for risk (t 2.37), and several fund forecasts, mostly of drawdowns (t 1.8 to 2.7). That period isn't a test, and those comparisons were never in a corrected family.
  • Fund prices are dividend-adjusted only to about late 2022, so fund results are reported and enter only the secondary tests.
  • One replication needed a guess. Samal et al. don't state the edge weights in their Forman curvature; the distance was used, it is labelled a guess, and it is reported only.
  • Choices made after results are recorded as such: a few reading aids (the A2 sign-flip null among them) and two bug fixes. None changed a rule or a verdict.

What would change the answer

  • Untouched data. The plan's real confirmation is on closes after 2026-10-19, which don't exist yet: the code frozen under a git tag, forecasts logged from each new price download before their outcomes exist, a first reading at 12 months and the deciding one at 36. It is pending: there is no tag yet, and the data accrues only if someone keeps downloading prices by hand, at least monthly. With every primary already failed, it would mostly record whether they keep failing.
  • Survivorship-free prices would settle the crisis-period question.
  • B4, the geodesic test on the macro state, waits for a statistical model linking the metric to the expected next state. The directional fund rotation runs only if a fund-level return forecast passes; it didn't (t −1.29).
