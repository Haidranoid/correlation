Alcohol Demand Test
For Luca Gaspari · tested 26 October 2026

Are less speculative stocks easier to forecast?

You suggested looking away from speculation and toward markets driven by fundamentals: the economy, social trends, age, new markets like cannabis and hard seltzer, and the news. I built that test for the alcohol industry and fixed every rule before looking at a result. The short answer: alcohol sales are forecastable, but alcohol stocks aren't. Their prices already move on the same information.

Your idea

"we are trying to predict markets that are influenced mostly by speculation, why not focusing on less speculative markets? … wine companies and consumptions are not influence much by speculations rather than by social trends, economy, age or opening of new markets like cannabis, hard seltzers, psychedelics … try to see if you can forecast those markets based on these factors + the news"Luca Gaspari, 26 October 2026

To be true in stock-market terms, this needs two things. Public information about alcohol demand would have to say something about next month's stock returns that prices don't already reflect. And it would have to work better for these "fundamental" companies than for speculative ones like the Nasdaq-100.

The companies

There is almost no listed wine company in the US to test. Duckhorn was taken private in 2024, Vintage Wine Estates went bankrupt the same year, and Willamette Valley Vineyards is too small to trade in size. Most wine is made by private firms like Gallo, or listed abroad. So the test uses the five US-listed alcohol producers whose biggest market is the US, equal-weighted and rebought every 21 trading days:

The six signals

Each is a 12-month change, so the strong seasons of drinking (December, summer, Dry January) cancel out. Each is used only once it was public: government data two months late, Google data one month late.

  1. E1The economy: the change in the unemployment rate.
  2. E2The economy: the change in consumer sentiment (University of Michigan).
  3. PPrices: alcohol's consumer prices against all prices. Rising faster means pricing power for producers.
  4. T1Social trends: Google searches for wine, beer, whiskey, vodka and tequila.
  5. T2Social trends: searches for drinking less: "non alcoholic", "sober", "mocktail", "dry january".
  6. T3New markets: searches for marijuana, cannabis, hard seltzer and psilocybin.

The forecast for each month is the average of six small forecasts, one per signal, each fitted only on months already over. Averaging keeps any single signal from dominating, a standard guard against fitting noise.

Google search interest in the US, monthly, each series scaled to its own peak (100). The trend you describe is real: searches about drinking less rose more than fivefold since 2005, while hard seltzer rose and faded within five years.

Did the signals forecast the stocks?

No. The question was whether the alcohol basket would beat the S&P 500 (SPY) over the next 21 trading days. Over 2008–2019, the period the rules judged, the signals' forecasts missed by more than the plain historical average did. The average is the forecast you get by knowing nothing.

None of the six worked on its own either. The best, searches for alcohol, scored −0.7% over 2008–2019. Putting all six into one regression, instead of averaging, scored −50%. That is what fitting noise looks like.

For scale: random made-up signals pushed through the same pipeline 400 times never reached the t ≥ 3 bar. A cheating "oracle" that knows next month's answer, blurred by noise four times its size, still passed easily (R² +13%). The test can detect a real signal.

Are alcohol stocks less speculative?

Not by how much they move. Less of their movement comes from the market as a whole, but what replaces it is company news: MGP Ingredients rose elevenfold in 2009 and fell 60% in 2024, and Brown-Forman fell 32% in 2024. And they weren't easier to forecast. The same pipeline run on other parts of the market came out about the same, near zero everywhere:

Demand is forecastable. The price already knows.

This part supports your intuition. Sales at US beer, wine and liquor stores follow trends you can forecast. Their growth three months ahead was predictable from recent growth (R² +10.5% over 2008–2019, +63.5% over 2020–2025), and over 2008–2019 the six signals added another 7.5%. The economy and social trends do drive consumption.

Twelve-month growth of sales at US beer, wine and liquor stores (Census, seasonally adjusted). The 2020 jump, when bars closed and people drank at home, lasted more than a year, which is why it was so forecastable.

But the stock market sees the same data, and the companies' own reports, before any outsider can trade on it. A trend that everyone can see is already in the price. That is why the stocks didn't follow the forecastable part of demand.

New markets: cannabis legalization

If cannabis replaces alcohol, alcohol stocks should fall when a state legalizes it. I fixed a list of 19 events beforehand: ballot measures, state laws, Canada's legalization and the two federal rescheduling steps. Over the next two days, the alcohol basket moved −0.2% against the S&P 500 on average (t −0.56), with the signs split evenly. Nothing.

The cannabis stocks themselves are the clearer lesson. On 30 April 2024, the report that the DEA would ease the rules lifted them 36% that day, and they gave back 21% over the next two. Over all 15 events since mid-2018, they rose 2.4% on the day and then fell 5.3% over the following month.

The news

I counted alcohol headlines in two archives the project already had: Reuters' US news (2007–2014, 13,580 alcohol headlines) and HuffPost (2012–2022, 1,279). Two signals came from them: how much of the news was about alcohol, and how positive it sounded. Neither helped. The share of alcohol headlines scored R² −6.2% and the tone −10.6%, and adding both to the six signals made the forecasts slightly worse. The keyword search also caught some false matches, like the Milwaukee Brewers.

Would it have made money?

The test's trading rule held the alcohol basket for the next 21 days when the forecast said it would beat the S&P 500, and the S&P 500 otherwise, paying 0.05% on every trade. Because the basket had beaten the market on average, the forecast nearly always said "alcohol": 94% of the time.

Growth of $1 from April 2008, after trading costs. The basket stayed ahead of the S&P 500 from 2009 to late 2025, mostly on MGP Ingredients' elevenfold 2009 rebound, though its lead shrank from 2018 on. Since mid-2025 it lost 17% while the S&P 500 gained 23%, and it now trails.
  • Against the S&P 500, to mid-2025: the rule beat it by 1.2 points a year after costs. That came from holding a basket picked with hindsight, not from the forecasts. Always holding the basket did better: 4.3 points a year.
  • Against random timing: shuffling the rule's own alcohol-or-S&P choices at random did better than the actual order 97.5% of the time. The timing cost 2.2 points a year.
  • In the 15 months nobody had seen: the rule lost 12.7%, the basket 17.3%, and the S&P 500 gained 23.1%.

Where your idea could still work

  • Information the market doesn't have yet. You work in the industry. Distributor depletions, retailer scanner data or what buyers tell you about next season arrive before the companies report. That kind of early, private-to-the-trade data is where demand knowledge can lead prices. Public statistics and Google searches don't.
  • Much longer horizons. DellaVigna and Pollet (2007) found that demand changes forecast from age, 5 to 10 years ahead, predicted industry returns across 48 industries, beer among them, because investors look about five years ahead at most. Shorter forecasts didn't work. Testing that needs many industries and decades, not one industry by the month.
  • Single companies around their earnings. Da, Engelberg and Gao found that rising Google searches for a company's own products predicted better-than-expected sales, and the stock moved when the sales were announced. That is a product-by-product test, a different design from this one.
  • Wine itself. Fine-wine prices are a real market, slower and thinner than stocks, and may suit a fundamentals view better. This project doesn't have that data. If you know of a public source, it could be tested the same way.

About this test

The rules were written and saved before any result was computed, and checked afterward for changes; there were none. A new idea needs a t-statistic of at least 3 over 2008–2019 to count. The period from 2020 to mid-2025 is a check, and the 15 months after it were run once, at the end. Three small changes were made during the run and are recorded: the strength of the cheating-oracle check, an added "event day" column, and one month left out between periods.

Data: Yahoo's adjusted closes (dividends included); unemployment and consumer prices from government series that aren't revised later; the University of Michigan's sentiment index; Google Trends downloaded on 26 October 2026; retail sales from the Census (revised later, so they were only reported, never used in the main test). Age wasn't tested: it changes once a year and, as DellaVigna and Pollet found, matters only at five- to ten-year horizons.

The plan, the code and the full write-up are in the project's public repository, github.com/bhoult/correlation (experiments/+1.2-luca-alcohol-demand.md and research/alcohol_demand/), with your permission.
