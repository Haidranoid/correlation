# Curved market space: can its geometry forecast the market, groups and funds?

**TL;DR:** Luca's idea: an aggregate (the market, a fund, a group) is more forecastable than its stocks, and the curvature of the market's correlation "space" helps forecast it. Over 2008–2019, simple price signals beat no level's historical mean, and forecastability didn't rise with basket size (p 0.38). Curvature, 92% of it plain graph statistics, added nothing to SPY's return or volatility forecasts, and log-Euclidean covariances built no calmer portfolios. A curvature timing rule matched random timing and trailed SPY by 3.05 points a year after costs. Verdict: no edge; all four primary tests fail.

## The hypothesis

Luca's two messages make two claims, tested separately:
- **A. Macro beats micro.** Computing every stock's exact state is a three-body problem, and impossible. That doesn't stop a good approximation of the whole: an aggregate (the market, a fund, a group) should be more forecastable than the stocks in it, the way temperature is forecastable and molecules aren't.
- **B. The market's space is curved, and the curvature matters.** Treat correlations as a metric, as general relativity treats the metric tensor. Its curvature should carry information about where aggregates go next.

B is A's tool. Luca's point is to forecast aggregates, so every geometry test below forecasts the market, groups or funds, not single stocks.

## What we already know

- **The macro state is forecastable. The macro direction hasn't been, so far.**
  - **State:** same-day co-movement repeats from one period to the next (`+0.0-lag-spectrum.md`, z = 83). The co-movement tools beat random choices out of sample (`+5.1-co-movement-tools.md`). Volatility-managed SPY was the best timing rule in `-3.2-market-timing.md`. This is the thermodynamics half of Luca's analogy, and it already works: the market's "temperature" (volatility, how tightly stocks move together) can be forecast.
  - **Direction:** every aggregate-level test here failed after costs, whether on the market (`-3.2-market-timing.md`, `-9.6-turn-of-month.md`), funds (`+1.2-trend-following.md`, a crisis hedge only; `-11.5-analog-days.md`) or groups (`+2.6-breadth-thrust.md`, `-4.5-cluster-lead-lag.md`, `-3.8-factor-momentum.md`).
  - None of them compared levels with the same forecaster. Test A does.
- **The three-body framing:** `pendulum/README.md` ("Where the metaphor breaks") argues that markets aren't low-dimensional chaos (Hsieh 1991). They are unpredictable mainly because news arrives at random and traders remove any pattern they can see. Averaging stocks cancels each company's own news, but not market-wide news, and market-wide news is what an aggregate's direction is made of. That is the main reason to expect A to hold for risk and fail for direction.
- **Market curvature is published,** as a crash-risk indicator (below). Whether it leads crashes or only marks them is disputed.

## Three geometries, not one

The word "geometry" covers three separate constructions here. Their distances, curvatures and geodesics are different objects, and a result in one says nothing about another:
- **G, the stock graph.** Nodes are stocks, edges link the most-correlated pairs, and an edge's length is d = √(2(1 − ρ)). Curvature means the discrete Ricci curvatures of that graph. Tests B1–B3.
- **M, the covariance manifold.** Points are covariance matrices, which must be positive definite. A demeaned 500-stock covariance from 63 days has rank at most 62, so every computation on M uses a shrunk (Ledoit-Wolf) estimate over one fixed list of stocks. Test B5.
  - **Log-Euclidean metric:** flat in matrix-log coordinates. It has no curvature at all.
  - **Affine-invariant metric:** curved.
- **S, the macro state space.** Points are (market return, volatility, average correlation), with a metric learned from how the state moves. Test B4, deferred.

General relativity supplies the vocabulary below, not a theory linking these three.

| In general relativity | Here | Computed as |
|---|---|---|
| Manifold | The space being described | G: stocks, as nodes of a graph. M: covariance matrices, positive definite only after shrinkage. S: the macro state |
| Metric tensor g | How far apart two things are | G: d = √(2(1 − ρ)) along an edge. Between two days' return vectors: the Mahalanobis form rᵀ Σ⁻¹ r, whose metric matrix is Σ⁻¹, so it needs an invertible, shrunk Σ. M: the log-Euclidean distance ‖log Σ₁ − log Σ₂‖ or the affine-invariant ‖log(Σ₁^−½ Σ₂ Σ₁^−½)‖, both on positive-definite matrices over the same stocks |
| Geodesic | The straightest path | G: shortest paths. M, affine-invariant: γ(s) = A^½ (A^−½ B A^−½)^s A^½ between aligned positive-definite matrices; one step past B gives B A⁻¹ B |
| Ricci curvature | Whether neighbouring paths converge or spread | G: Ollivier-Ricci, κ(x, y) = 1 − W₁(m_x, m_y) / d(x, y), comparing two linked stocks' neighbourhoods by optimal transport. Triangle-augmented Forman curvature, F(x, y) = 4 − deg x − deg y + 3 × (triangles on the edge). Positive: a tight group that moves as one. Negative: a bridge between groups |
| Geodesic deviation | How fast nearby paths separate | The pendulum's doubling time, in geometric language (Casetti et al.) |
| Stress-energy tensor ("matter tells space how to curve") | What bends the structure | Money: dollar volume, index and fund flows. Index inclusion adds co-movement (Barberis, Shleifer & Wurgler 2005, in `prior-work.md` §4) |
| Einstein's field equations | The law linking the two | None known. This is the missing piece |

### Where the analogy breaks

- **No field equation.** In general relativity, matter determines the metric by a law. Here the metric is estimated from noisy data and no law says how money bends it. Measuring curvature isn't the same as having a theory of it.
- **The metric is mostly estimation.** A 500 × 500 covariance from 63 days is singular, with about 125,000 numbers estimated from about 31,500 returns. Shrinkage makes it usable, and noise has curvature too.
- **Delays are bounded only at daily resolution.** Changes in gravity travel at a finite speed. The market analogue, a move in A that reaches B later, is what the census looked for. On daily closes, at move lengths of 1–63 days and offsets up to two years, it found lagged correlations at about the rate chance produces (`+2.0-correlation-census.md`). That bounds such delays at that resolution. It doesn't rule out delays too weak for the census to detect on single pairs, delays faster than a day, or ones that changed sign between periods.
- **Planets don't read forecasts.** Traders do, and a curvature that predicted returns would be traded until it stopped (`pendulum/README.md`, "The market fights back").

## Rules (proposed; fixed before any result is seen)

### Timeline, dates and costs

- **Decisions** are made after the month-end close t₀ (every 21 trading days on the fixed calendar), from data through that close only. **Trades** happen at the close of t₀ + 1 (primary). Trading at the close of t₀, as `-3.2-market-timing.md` did, is reported as an upper bound.
- **Every fit** uses only data through its decision date, on an expanding window. Universe, twins and groups are taken as of t₀.
- **Dates:**
  - **first data:** the export starts 2001-12-31. The first 63-day state is in 2002-04, and `c2eval.twins` (needed for the exclusions) first exists in 2003-01;
  - **monthly rules (B3):** a trailing median needs 24 earlier month-ends (`trailing_median` in `research/market_timing/common.py`: the previous 60, at least 24), so the rules start in 2005-04, as `-3.2` did;
  - **forecast regressions (A, B2):** at least 60 monthly observations before the first scored month, so scoring runs 2008-01 to 2019-12;
  - **covariance tests (B5):** need 252 days per window, so they run 2006 to 2019, as `-4.6-correlation-portfolios.md` did.
- **Cash** earns the T-bill (`pubdata.rf_daily`) in every rule and baseline. Cash at 0 is also reported, for comparison with `-3.2`.
- **Costs:** 5 bps a side on every traded dollar. Each money test also reports 20 bps, turnover, volatility and maximum drawdown.

### Holdouts: what each period has already been exposed to

- **2006–2019, development:** every earlier experiment has read it.
- **2020–2025-06:** in the same export, and read by nearly every experiment. The yardsticks here (average correlation, the absorption ratio, volatility management, Ledoit-Wolf minimum variance) were chosen knowing how they did in it. It is a chronological evaluation period, not a holdout.
- **The 16 unseen months (2025-06-30 to 2026-10-19):** each earlier experiment read them once, about 65 in all, and they shaped the conclusions that shaped this plan. They are still run once at the end and reported as used.
- **Untouched: closes after 2026-10-19,** which don't exist yet.
  - The code and parameters are frozen under a git tag, and forecasts are logged from each new Stooq download before their outcomes exist.
  - **Confirmation:** each primary test is confirmed at one-sided p < 0.05 after Holm's correction across the four. These tests are monthly, so 12 months gives only 12 observations, too few to confirm alone. The first reading is at 12 months and the deciding one at 36.
  - Until then, any pass is labelled exploratory.
  - **This depends on downloads.** Stooq downloads are manual (a browser and a CAPTCHA), and the weekday update timer is off. The data accrues only if someone keeps downloading, at least monthly, with `c2 update` run on each.

### Statistics

- **Comparisons are paired,** over non-overlapping periods.
- **Uncertainty:** Newey-West standard errors with 2 periods of lag, and a moving-block bootstrap that resamples whole dates (blocks of 6 months, 10,000 draws).
- **Nested forecasts** (a model against the same model plus one more input: A's calibrated forecasts against the historical mean, and B2): Clark-West.
- **Non-nested forecasts** (B5's covariance forecasts, and B4 if it is ever run): paired loss differences (Diebold-Mariano) with the same Newey-West errors.
- **Every comparison reports** the actual out-of-sample loss improvement with a 90% interval, not only whether it passes.
- **Bars:**
  - **primary tests (A1, B2 direction, B2 risk, B5):** t ≥ 3 each (Newey-West), the project's bar for a new idea, which is stricter than Holm's correction across four tests would need. The bootstrap p is reported beside each;
  - **secondary tests:** Holm's correction at a family level of 0.05, one-sided.
- **Each test gets its own conclusion.** None depends on another passing.
- **Why the correction:** ten independent tests at 5% would give 0.5 false passes on average and about a 40% chance of at least one. These tests are neither independent nor at one level, so the file controls the risk by naming its primaries, correcting the secondaries, and confirming on untouched data.

### A. The aggregation ladder (no geometry)

**Two separate hypotheses:**
- **A1 (primary). Aggregation helps:** time-varying predictability rises with the size of the basket.
- **A2 (secondary). Structure helps:** co-moving groups beat random baskets matched to them. A1 doesn't need A2.

**Levels,** rebuilt at each t₀ from the 500 most-traded stocks (minus `c2eval.excluded(E, tw)`):
1. single stocks;
2. random equal-weight baskets of 5, 25 and 100, 1,000 of each per period, drawn uniformly (for A1);
3. **groups:** the stocks sharing a twin fund (`c2eval.twins`), equal-weighted, at least 5 stocks;
4. **matched random baskets (for A2):** for each group member, a non-member drawn at random from the same cell of a 5 × 5 grid of market beta and volatility (both over the trailing 252 days, as of t₀). This matches size, beta and volatility. Sector can't be matched without rebuilding the group itself;
5. twin funds, on their own prices;
6. the export's equal-weight `market`, and SPY.

**Signals:**
- **trend:** the 12-month return skipping the last month, as a value, not its sign;
- **reversal:** minus the last 5-day return;
- **AR(1):** the last return of the horizon's length;
- **volatility:** the last 21- and 63-day log realized volatility.

**From signal to forecast:**
- Every member's signal and outcome are divided by its own trailing 63-day volatility before pooling, so volatile members don't dominate.
- Each signal x becomes a return forecast a + b·x by least squares on the level's pooled pairs whose outcomes ended before the decision. The volatility forecast is a two-term HAR fitted the same way.
- No future data enters any fit.

**Benchmarks:**
- **the historical mean** of the level's scaled outcomes, to date. R² against it measures time-varying predictability, the claim.
- **zero.** R² against zero is reported, but it mixes in drift. If returns are a constant μ plus unpredictable noise, forecasting μ beats zero more and more as averaging removes noise, though nobody predicts anything.

**Direction, evaluated separately:**
- the hit rate of the signal's sign against the outcome minus its historical mean;
- the rank correlation between signal and outcome across a level's members each period.

**Horizons:** 5 and 21 days. One day is skipped between the decision close and the start of the move, so stale prices in thin stocks can't make baskets look predictable (Lo & MacKinlay's non-synchronous trading).

**A1 pass:**
- for at least one calibrated return forecaster, the slope of out-of-sample R² (against the historical mean) on log basket size, over single stocks, the random baskets and the market, is positive over 2008–2019, with its date-bootstrap one-sided p below 0.0013 (the level t ≥ 3 implies);
- and the market level's R² is above zero by Clark-West.

**A2 pass:** groups' R² above their matched baskets', under Holm's correction among the secondaries.

**The control:** the same for the volatility forecaster. It is expected to pass, which shows the ladder can detect something.

**If A1 passes,** that level's forecaster gets a money test with its own stated rule, written into this file before running.

### B. The stock graph G

**The curvature series,** built once:
- **Universe and window:** as `states()` in `research/market_timing/common.py`. Every 21 trading days, the 500 most-traded stocks minus `c2eval.excluded`, daily log returns over the last 63 days.
- **Graph (primary):** edges between the 5% most-correlated pairs (6,237 of 124,750).
  - Fixing the edge count removes changing density.
  - It doesn't make the graph's shape independent of the level of correlation, factor loadings or estimation error. That is what the controls below are for.
- **Graph (reported):** the published style, edges above a fixed correlation threshold, the value read from Sandhu et al. and Samal et al. before running.
- **Triangle-augmented Forman curvature,** averaged over edges, has an exact form: F̄ = 4 − Σ_v d_v² ÷ m + 9T ÷ m, where m is the number of edges and T the number of triangles.
  - At a fixed edge count, it is entirely set by degree concentration and triangle count. It is reported beside those two numbers.
  - Before running, check which Forman version Samal et al. used. If theirs differs (a weighted form, say), theirs is run too, as the replication.
- **Ollivier-Ricci:** κ(x, y) = 1 − W₁(m_x, m_y) ÷ d(x, y). m_x puts half its mass on x and spreads the rest evenly over x's neighbours. d = √(2(1 − ρ)), with shortest paths on the graph. Averaged over edges.
- **Plain graph statistics, reported beside the curvatures:**
  - degree concentration (Σ d² ÷ m);
  - triangle density (T ÷ m);
  - the edges' average correlation;
  - the largest connected component's share of stocks.
- **Group curvature (for B2's fund targets):** for each twin group, the average over edges with both ends in the group of each edge's curvature, computed on the full graph.
  - A group with fewer than 10 internal edges gets no value.
  - Each group's series is standardized against its own earlier month-ends (the previous 60, at least 24), so groups of different sizes are compared by change rather than level.
  - The group's internal edge count and average correlation go in as controls.
- **Which sign means fragile** is taken from the papers before any test is read.

**Checks, each for a different question.** None of them shows forecasting value; only B2 does.
- **Is there shared structure at all?** The same pipeline after shifting each stock's returns by its own random number of days. Rejecting this only shows stocks share structure.
- **Is it more than the market and sectors?** Rebuild each stock's returns as its fitted market and twin-fund loadings times the real factor returns, plus its own residuals shifted by a random number of days, then recompute. The real curvature minus this curvature is the part ordinary factors don't produce.
- **Is it more than degrees?** Rewire the real graph by double-edge swaps that keep every stock's degree and the graph simple, then recompute. Forman's average is fixed by degrees and triangles, so this tests the triangles. For Ollivier it tests everything beyond degrees.
  - **Why swaps are a fair null:** the swap chain reaches a uniform sample of graphs with the given degrees for every degree sequence ("Polynomial mixing of the switch chain for every graphical degree sequence", family 131 of OpenAI's 2026 [`openai/math`](https://github.com/openai/math) collection; machine-checked in Lean, not peer-reviewed).
  - **How many swaps:** that proof's bound, on the order of n⁸ steps, is far too loose to set a number. Swap in rounds of m, at least 10 rounds, and stop once the average triangle count over the last 5 rounds is within one standard deviation of the 5 before.
- **Is it stable?** Curvature from the window's odd days against its even days, correlated across months; and the same series on 252-day windows.

**B1. What is curvature made of? (descriptive)**
- Regress each curvature series on average correlation, the absorption ratio and SPY's 63-day realized volatility, and separately on the plain graph statistics. Report R² and the residual's spread.
- Neither a high nor a low R² decides anything. A large shared part can still leave a useful remainder, and a small one can be noise. B2 decides.

**B2 (primary). Does curvature add to forecasts?** This is the dispute between Samal et al. and arXiv 2405.07134.
- **Targets,** over the 21 trading days after the trade date (closes t₀ + 1 to t₀ + 22):
  - **direction (primary):** SPY's excess return;
  - **risk (primary):** SPY's log realized volatility;
  - **reported:** SPY's maximum drawdown, and for each twin group's fund, the same three.
- **Models,** nested and fitted by least squares on an expanding window:
  1. **baseline:** the last 21- and 63-day realized volatility, average correlation and the absorption ratio (for a fund: its own realized volatilities and its group's average correlation);
  2. baseline + plain graph statistics;
  3. baseline + plain graph statistics + Ollivier curvature;
  4. baseline + Forman curvature. Forman can't add anything beyond model 2, being an exact function of its statistics at a fixed edge count, so it is tested against the baseline only and read as "graph statistics help", not "curvature helps".
- **The key comparison:** model 3 against model 2, by Clark-West, scored 2008–2019.
- **Pass:** t ≥ 3 for the direction target, or for the risk target. These are two conclusions, each named. The other targets are reported under Holm's correction among the secondaries.
- **Reported:** curvature around every SPY drawdown of 15% or more, and when it moved relative to the peak.

**B3 (secondary). Money tests.**
- **Market allocation rule:** hold 100% SPY when curvature is on the calm side of its own trailing median (the previous 60 month-ends, at least 24), and 50% otherwise. Decided at t₀ and traded at the close of t₀ + 1, with cash at the T-bill. Reuse `research/market_timing/engine.py` with a curvature column added to `states()`.
  - **Baselines** (paired, same timing, costs and cash):
    - a constant allocation at the rule's own average SPY share, so lower exposure can't pass for timing;
    - a volatility threshold with the same two levels: 100% when SPY's 21-day realized volatility is below its trailing median, otherwise 50%;
    - volatility-managed SPY at the same average exposure;
    - the average-correlation and absorption rules from `-3.2`, rerun with this timing and cash;
    - 10,000 random timings with the same months, reported (the file-name number).
  - **Pass:** a positive paired net difference against the constant allocation, the volatility threshold, and the better of the two `-3.2` rules.
- **Fund risk scaling:** a long-only portfolio of the twin funds that have a group value, weighted by 1 ÷ forecast volatility. Three versions differ only in the forecast: B2's fund model with group curvature, the same without it, and plain trailing 63-day volatility. Compare realized portfolio volatility and Sharpe ratio, paired. This is risk management and makes no claim about direction.
  - **Pass:** the curvature version's realized volatility below the no-curvature version's, paired, under Holm's correction among the secondaries. Its Sharpe ratio is reported.
- **A directional fund rotation is not run as written.** Buying calm funds and shorting fragile ones assumes that risk predicts direction, but riskier funds may earn more, and such a book mostly buys sector and beta exposures. It runs only if B2's fund-level return forecast passes. It would then rank funds by that forecast, hedge each leg's market beta with SPY, and get its own hypothesis written into this file first.

**B4. Do markets follow geodesics? (deferred)**

The most literal reading of "space tells matter how to move", and the least specified. As first written, it fitted a metric g(x) to the local covariance of moves in the macro state x = (the equal-weight market's trailing 21-day return, its log realized volatility, average correlation). It then integrated the geodesic equation from today's state and last move.

It runs only after A, B2 and the pilot, with these written into this file first:
- **A statistical model linking the metric to the expected next state.** A local diffusion covariance can motivate a metric. It doesn't make the expected future state follow a geodesic, and most-likely-path arguments hold only over short times and with qualifications. Geodesic motion is an assumed forecasting rule, not a consequence.
- **A justification for starting from the last move,** which assumes velocity persists in a noisy system.
- **A minimum effective number of past states inside the kernel.** With about 200 monthly points by 2019, a 3-dimensional neighbourhood of 0.5 standard deviations holds very few.
- **Fitting details:** regularization of g, coordinate scaling on training data only, the finite-difference step, the integration units, and a fall-back to "no change" outside well-supported regions.
- **The comparison:** paired forecast loss (Diebold-Mariano) against no change, a straight line and AR(1), because these aren't nested models.

### B5 (primary). The covariance manifold M: does the representation matter?

- **Alignment:** at each t₀, the stocks of MV-LW's universe in `-4.6-correlation-portfolios.md` (the 500 most-traded, with returns on at least 90% of t₀ − 251 … t₀). Every covariance window and the next 21 days' target use that same list, so a matrix row is the same company everywhere. Nothing is compared across different months' lists. Missing returns are handled as MV-LW handles them.
- **Each window's covariance,** over 21, 63 and 252 days, is Ledoit-Wolf shrunk toward constant correlation, which makes it positive definite.
- **Forecasts of the next 21 days' covariance:**
  - **LW-252:** MV-LW's existing estimator;
  - **Euclidean combination:** w₁ S₂₁ + w₂ S₆₃ + w₃ S₂₅₂;
  - **log-Euclidean combination:** exp(w₁ log S₂₁ + w₂ log S₆₃ + w₃ log S₂₅₂).
- **Weights:** on the simplex (each at least 0, summing to 1), in steps of 0.1 (66 choices). Each month, the choice with the lowest average loss over all earlier months is used. Until 12 months of loss history exist, the weights are equal (⅓ each), and scoring starts after those 12 months. The two combinations share the grid, windows, shrinkage, loss and fitting. Only the representation differs.
- **Loss (primary):** the realized variance, over the next 21 days, of the capped minimum-variance portfolio built from the forecast, with MV-LW's 2% cap and solver. QLIKE against the next 21 days' shrunk covariance is reported.
- **Pass:** log-Euclidean's loss below Euclidean's, paired, t ≥ 3 over 2006–2019. Both are also reported against LW-252.
- **What a pass shows:** the log-Euclidean metric is flat, so a pass supports that representation for forecasting. It doesn't show that market curvature predicts anything.
- **An adaptation, not a replication:**
  - Bucci, Palma & Zhang forecast realized covariance matrices; here the inputs are rolling covariances of daily closes;
  - the weights here are chosen on a fixed grid, not estimated as their model does;
  - the universe here is 500 stocks, not a small set of assets.
- **Reported, not pass/fail:** the affine-invariant step B A⁻¹ B, with A and B the shrunk 63-day covariances ending 21 days apart, both on the current list. It is valid mathematics for aligned positive-definite matrices. That doesn't make it statistically stable or economically justified, and the file says which it turns out to be.

## Ways it can fool us

- **Curvature is simpler statistics renamed.** Average Forman curvature is exactly degree concentration and triangle count. Hence the plain graph statistics in B2's nested models, and the rewiring check.
- **Curvature of noise.** A 500-stock covariance from 63 days is singular. Hence shrinkage wherever a matrix is inverted or logged, and the stability checks.
- **Misaligned matrices.** Combining or comparing covariances whose rows are different companies mixes coordinate systems. Hence B5's same-list rule.
- **Few independent events.** Twenty years hold only a handful of large drawdowns, and a crash indicator can look perfect on that few. Paired tests with the date bootstrap are the yardstick.
- **Choices made after looking:** network type, density, the Ollivier mass split, the fragile sign, the weight grid. All are fixed above. Anything changed later is recorded as made after results.
- **Marking the crash it sits in.** A 63-day window that ends in a crash shows the crash, so only targets after the trade date count.
- **Stale prices make baskets look predictable.** Hence the skipped day in A.
- **Drift that looks like predictability.** Hence R² against the historical mean, not just against zero.
- **Survivor-only data.**
  - The stocks are those still listed, so firms that failed, such as Lehman Brothers in 2008, are missing. That can change the correlation structure in exactly the crises where curvature is meant to matter.
  - Forecasting the real SPY series doesn't repair a predictor built from biased data.
  - Survivorship-free data (Sharadar, already an open decision in the README) would settle it. Until then, every crisis-period result is flagged as possibly biased.

## What would count as Luca being right

Each test answers its own question:
- **A1:** time-varying predictability rises with aggregation, against the historical mean and not only against zero. **A2:** economically linked groups add to that beyond matched random baskets.
- **B2, direction:** curvature adds to the market's return forecast beyond the yardsticks and the plain graph statistics. This is the closest test of Luca's geometric claim.
- **B2, risk; B3's fund risk scaling:** graph features help forecast risk. That is useful, but it is a different claim from Luca's.
- **B3's market rule:** support for one allocation rule, no more.
- **B5:** support for a covariance representation, not for curvature.
- **No pass would show** that relativity's equations, or any law like them, govern markets.

### Hypothesis A: is an aggregate more forecastable than its stocks?

**Definitions used throughout:**
- **R²** is out-of-sample R² against the level's historical mean, in percent: 1 − Σ(outcome − forecast)² ÷ Σ(outcome − historical mean)², pooled over a level's members and the period's months (Campbell and Thompson's). Above 0 means the forecaster beat the historical mean.
  - **Outcome:** the log return from the close of t₀ + 1 to the close of t₀ + 1 + h, for h = 5 or 21 days, divided by the member's trailing 63-day volatility.
  - **Forecast:** a + b × signal, fitted by least squares on all the level's earlier pairs.
- **The slope:** the least-squares slope of the five levels' R² (single stocks, baskets of 5, 25 and 100, and the export's equal-weight market of about 1,290 stocks) on ln(size), in R² points per unit of ln size. From one stock to the market is 7.16 units, so a slope of +0.10 would put the market's R² about 0.7 points above a single stock's.
- **Bootstrap p:** a moving-block bootstrap of decision months, blocks of 6, 10,000 draws, one-sided: the share of draws with a slope ≤ 0.
- **The test window:** 144 decision months, 2008-01..2019-12, on the old export. Run on numpy only, about 3½ minutes of compute in all.

**The tests, in order** (the verdict stops at the first failure; every number is still computed):
1. **A1, the slope: fails.** No forecaster's slope is positive with p < 0.0013. Four of the five are negative. The only positive one, AR(1) at 21 days, is +0.078 (90% interval −0.38 to +0.49), p 0.38.
2. **A1, the market above zero by Clark-West: fails.** The best is AR(1) at 21 days, t +0.71 (needed ≥ 3). No forecaster would pass even at 1.645.
3. **The control, the volatility forecaster: fails its slope.** Its R² is positive at every level, from +4.1% for single stocks to +7.4% for 100-baskets at 5 days. But the market's +6.6% sits below the 100-baskets, and the slope's p is 0.25 (21 days: 0.35), though its market Clark-West passes (t 3.17 and 3.29). So the ladder as the plan specifies it failed to detect even the effect the plan expected it to detect. That failed sanity check limits how much A1's failure can say about a small effect. The return R²s aren't small and positive, though: they are below 0.
4. **A2, groups above matched baskets: not passed.** Groups beat their matched random baskets for all five forecasters, by +0.09 to +1.31 R² points. The best, trend at 21 days, has p 0.017 raw, 0.084 after Holm across the five and 0.185 after Holm across all 11 secondaries, and both groups' and baskets' R² are negative.
