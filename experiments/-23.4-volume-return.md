# Volume and return: do heavy-volume moves continue where informed traders dominate?

**TL;DR:** Llorente, Michaely, Saar and Wang (2002) found that a big move on heavy volume reverses less, or even continues, in small stocks where informed traders matter more. On surviving US stocks in 2008–2019 their own test fails: the least-traded third's volume effect beat the most-traded third's by 0.006, t 0.7 (t ≥ 2 needed). Across stocks the pattern does show on the next day (t −6.9), but it is gone by the day after, the first day a trader who must wait for the day's volume can trade, so adding it to a reversal book lost 1.5 bps a day and the daily 20-stock account trailed SPY by 23.4 points a year after costs. Verdict: no edge.

## The claim

**From the paper.** I read the NYU working paper of 2000-08-15; the published tables may differ slightly.
- **The model:** investors trade either to hedge or to speculate on private information.
  - A price move caused by hedging trades reverses, because nothing about the company changed.
  - A move caused by speculative trades continues, as the private information comes out.
  - Volume marks the days when trading, not public news, moved the price.
- **The test:** NYSE and AMEX common stocks with at least 1,000 trading days in 1993–1998 (2,226 stocks). For each stock, one daily time-series regression:
  - R(t+1) = C0 + C1·R(t) + C2·V(t)·R(t);
  - V(t) is log turnover (plus 0.00000255) minus its average over the previous 200 days.
- **The results** by thirds of market value (Table 3):

| Third by market value | Mean market value | C1 | C2 | Stocks with C2 < 0 | Stocks with \|t(C2)\| > 1.64 | sd of V |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Small | $86M | −0.104 | +0.030 | 167 of 742 | 429 | 1.54 |
| Middle | $526M | +0.005 | +0.005 | 355 | 284 | 1.00 |
| Large | $7.2B | +0.003 | +0.001 | 354 | 222 | 0.67 |

- **Regressing C2 on rank:**
  - on the rank of size, the slope is −0.044 (t −12.4, R² 6.4%);
  - on the rank of the bid-ask spread, it is +0.056 (t 15.9, R² 10.2%);
  - by spread thirds, C2 is +0.035 for the widest spreads and −0.003 for the narrowest.
- **Robustness in the paper:** autocorrelated errors, trimmed outliers, mid-quote returns (against bid-ask bounce), longer intervals for thinly traded stocks, ten years, analyst counts and market-model residuals.
- **The page's summary:** "A big move on heavy volume continues over the next days in stocks where informed traders dominate, and reverses where the trading is mostly hedging or noise."

**What the paper's numbers say, read plainly:**
- **The effect weakens reversal; it rarely turns it into continuation.**
  - In the small third, C1 + C2·V is positive only when V > 3.5, which is volume 32 times its usual level. V's sd there is 1.5.
  - So most heavy-volume days in small stocks still reverse, just less. The page's "continues" holds only on the most extreme days.
- **Large stocks' C2 is about zero (+0.001), not negative.** Only the narrowest-spread third is negative, at −0.003. "Reverses in large stocks" is the weaker half of the claim.
- **It is all about the next day.** The paper doesn't test day 2 or later.
- **The sample is old:** 1993–1998, before decimal prices (2001), off-exchange trading and high-frequency market making.

**Novelty and background check (2026-10-25; web searches before any data was touched; it changed no rules):** partly done.
- **The original:** the details above come from the 2000 working paper.
- **Out of sample:** Wang, Qian & Wang (2018, *International Review of Financial Analysis* 58, 195–210) test aggregate volume out of sample.
  - Higher returns follow heavier volume in US data, mostly before 2000.
  - The economic gain is small, a 0.73% a year certainty equivalent.
  - The effect is absent in most other major markets.
  - It is a market-level test, not the paper's cross-section.
- **Other markets:** a master's thesis at National Chengchi University reports the paper's pattern on Taiwan's exchange with several proxies.
- **Another explanation:** Miwa & Ueda (2011, "A dynamic volume–return relation and investors' positive feedback trading") argue feedback trading can produce the relation without news. From a search summary; not read here.
- **Not found:** a test of the paper's cross-sectional pattern on US stocks after 2008, or a trading version of it measured against reversal.

## The four design choices

### 1. The information proxy, the volume measure and the split

- **Proxy: dollar volume.** The stock's median daily dollar volume over the 252 days through the calendar day, ranked within the universe and cut into thirds.
  - It is the nearest stand-in available for size (no shares outstanding) and for spreads. In the paper, the ranks of size and spread correlate at −0.88.
  - **It also measures liquidity.** The paper argues liquidity predicts the opposite sign: thin stocks should reverse more after heavy volume, from price pressure. So mistaking one for the other works against the claim.
  - Three other proxies are reported only: Amihud's illiquidity, idiosyncratic volatility and the Corwin–Schultz spread from highs and lows.
- **Volume: share volume, not dollar volume.**
  - Detrended log dollar volume = detrended log share volume + (log price − its 200-day mean).
  - The second part is a slow momentum term, and multiplied by R(t) it would make an interaction of its own.
  - Share volume = the export's dollar volume ÷ its close. Dollar volume is unaffected by splits (`stockai/PLAN.md`), so this is split-adjusted share volume, and the dividend factor cancels.
  - Detrended log share volume is close to the paper's detrended log turnover, since shares outstanding move slowly over 200 days (issuance and buybacks aside).
  - **Detrending, the paper's:** log volume minus its mean over the previous 200 days.
- **Split: thirds, not per-stock estimates.**
  - Per-stock C2 from a training window is too noisy. With 1,516 days the paper found |t| > 1.64 for only 30–58% of stocks per group, and a two-year window has a third of the data.
  - Per-stock estimates are a reported variant.

### 2. The tradable primary (M1)

- **The score** is the paper's regression fitted point in time, separately in each third: b_g·R + v_g·V + c_g·V·R.
- **The comparison** is the same model without V·R. Both use R(t) and V(t) with the same group-specific fit, so the difference is the part beyond r(t) alone, with r(t) and V(t) as controls. Plain reversal is also graded on the same cells.
- **Timing:** V(t) needs the whole day's volume, known only after the close. So trades are at the close of t + 1 (the judge's `delay=1`), and the target is R(t + 2).
  - That is a day later than anything the paper tested.
  - The paper's own timing (trading at the close of t) and a weekly version are reported, not pass/fail.
- **The judge** grades the same score for the two numbers in the index. M1's pass rests on the paired decile books, not on the judge, because the books give a daily series the date-block bootstrap can test, while the judge gives 18 yearly numbers.
- **"Beat plain reversal" is read as** beating the same fitted model without V·R, which holds both r(t) and V(t). The comparison with plain −R is reported, paired, but isn't a pass condition.

### 3. The replication (R1)

- **The paper's method:** a per-stock time-series regression, here per calendar year, and C2 compared between the lowest and highest thirds by dollar volume.
- **It is a second primary, under Holm,** since it is the claim itself.
- **Standard errors:** a date-block bootstrap shared across stocks, so the cross-correlation the paper's t of −12 ignores is kept.

### 4. The universe, and how survivors bias it

- **R1 uses a wide universe** (median close ≥ $5, median dollar volume ≥ $250,000).
  - The paper's small third traded about $150,000–$310,000 a day (median and mean, Table 1, 1993–98 dollars), about $250,000–$500,000 in 2008–2019 dollars.
  - The judge's $5M floor would cut out most of the stocks where the paper found C2 > 0.
- **M1 uses the judge-like liquid set,** where the judge can trade and 5 bps a side is realistic. Below $5M nothing can produce a judge number.
- **Survivors bias R1 mostly against the claim** (details under "Ways it can fool us"). A small company whose heavy-volume fall kept going to delisting is missing; the survivors are those whose falls reversed.

## Rules (proposed; fixed before any result is seen)

### Data and timing

- **Exports**, checked 2026-10-25 (existence only):
  - old: 8,503 stocks × 5,911 days, 2001-12-31..2025-06-27, fingerprint 8b5ac076267c83ab, arrays `close`, `dollar_volume`, `v`, `market`;
  - new: 9,508 × 6,240, through 2026-10-19, fingerprint 5e2aaa6254c435e6;
  - the twins caches exist for both.
  - The store lists 4,744 Nasdaq, 3,443 NYSE and 316 NYSE American stocks, and 3,027 funds.
- **Closes:** the export's Stooq closes, split- and dividend-adjusted, after correlation2's bad-print repair.
  - The repair looks at the next close: a known small look-ahead (`experiments/README.md`).
  - Dividends are back-adjusted through about October–November 2022 and raw after.
- **Returns:** simple, R(t) = C(t) ÷ C(t − 1) − 1. A return is missing if either close is missing, and treated as a bad print (missing) if |R| > 0.5.
- **Share volume:** sv(t) = dollar volume(t) ÷ close(t), missing if either is missing or ≤ 0.
- **Thin early data:** before 2005-02-25 the export holds about 320 stocks, so the universes fill from 2006.
- **2011-02-17:** the export has closes for about 571 of 2,530 stocks. That day's returns and volumes are missing, with no fill, and the affected pairs drop out.
- **Decisions:** everything known at day t uses data through the close of t.
  - V(t) uses the whole day's volume, so any trade is at the close of t + 1 and earns R(t + 2).
  - R1 is a description, not a trade, and uses R(t + 1) as the paper does.

### V, detrended volume

- **V(t) = ln sv(t) − the mean of ln sv over the valid days among t − 200..t − 1,** needing at least 160 valid days.
- **Broken prints:** a day with sv below 1% of the median sv over t − 200..t − 1 is missing, and isn't used in later windows.
  - This is built for the new store's broken volumes around 2026-03-03 (a few dozen shares for about 700 stocks).
  - Counts by year are reported.
- **Zero or missing volume** is missing; the paper's small constant isn't needed.
- **Clip:** V is clipped at ±5 as a guard. How often it binds is reported.

### The proxy and the thirds

- **Calendar:** each calendar day t0 of `c2eval.twins` (export days 252 + 21k).
- **Proxy:** each stock's median dollar volume over the valid days among the 252 through t0.
- **G** is its percentile in the universe at t0: rank with ties averaged, (rank − 0.5) ÷ n.
- **Thirds:** low (G < 1/3, the most information asymmetry), middle, high (G ≥ 2/3). They apply to days t0..t0 + 20.

### Universes

At each calendar day t0, applying to days t0..t0 + 20:
- **U_W, the wide universe (R1's):**
  - closes on ≥ 90% of the 252 days through t0, and positive share volume on ≥ 90%;
  - median close ≥ $5 and median dollar volume ≥ $250,000 over those days;
  - not `c2eval.excluded(E, tw)` at t0.
  - **Why $5:** bid-ask bounce. The paper checked bounce with mid-quotes, which this project doesn't have. Bounce is largest in low-priced stocks, and it pushes C2 the claim's way in thin stocks (see "Ways it can fool us").
- **U_J, the judge-like liquid set (M1's):** the same, with median dollar volume ≥ $5M. It is formula mining's point-in-time version of the judge's rule.
  - The judge adds its own census-variable history rule on top, which leaves about 300 names in 2007–08.
- **Reported:**
  - the 500 largest of U_J by 63-day median dollar volume, ranked within the 500;
  - U_W with a $1 floor instead of $5.
- **The floors were set without counting stocks;** nothing has been computed on the data. Expected sizes, from earlier files:
  - the judge-like set with a twin had a median of 1,103 stocks a week over 2008–2019 (`+2.5`);
  - stocks ≥ $5, not fund-like and with a twin numbered 1,759 in 2008 and 4,599 in 2024 (`+1.1`).
  - The actual sizes are reported first.

### R1 (primary). Is C2 higher where dollar volume is lowest?

- **Stock-years:** each calendar year y, 2008..2019.
  - A stock enters year y if it is in U_W on the calendar day in force on y's first trading day, and has at least 200 valid pairs in y.
  - A pair is a day t in y with R(t), V(t) and R(t + 1) all valid; t + 1 may fall in y + 1.
- **The paper's regression,** OLS per stock-year: R(t + 1) = C0 + C1·R(t) + C2·V(t)·R(t).
- **Thirds:** by G on that calendar day, re-ranked among the stock-years in the year's regressions.
- **The statistic:**
  - Δ_y = mean C2 of the low third − mean C2 of the high third;
  - C2 is winsorized at the year's 1st and 99th percentiles across all of that year's stocks;
  - R1 = the mean of Δ_2008..Δ_2019, with the years weighted equally.
  - **The claim says R1 > 0.**
- **Not point in time, and it doesn't need to be:** R1 describes, it doesn't trade. The 200-pair rule uses the year's own days.

### M1 (primary). Does the volume × return term add to a reversal book?

Universe U_J; daily decisions; trades a day late.
- **Features at the close of t:**
  - R(t), V(t) and the third g;
  - R and V winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles within each third on each day.
- **Target:** y = R(t + 2), the return of a position bought at the close of t + 1 and sold at the close of t + 2. Missing if either close is missing.
- **Daily fits,** within each third g on each origin day s with at least 100 stocks having every value:
  - full: y on [1, R, V, V·R];
  - base: y on [1, R, V].
- **Coefficients** used on days t0..t0 + 20:
  - the means of the daily estimates over origins s with s + 2 ≤ t0, so that every target's last close is in by t0. The code asserts it on every fit;
  - expanding from the first origin, and at least 252 origins are needed;
  - the first fit is on the last calendar day before 2008-01-02, from origins in about 2006–2007 (about 480);
  - expanding rather than rolling, because the claim is a stable property of how a stock trades, and the paper used six years.
- **Scores,** with no intercepts:
  - P_full = b̂_g·R + v̂_g·V + ĉ_g·V·R;
  - P_base = b̂′_g·R + v̂′_g·V.
- **The coefficients follow the data.** If ĉ_g's order across the thirds comes out opposite to the paper's, the score trades what the data says. R1 tests the paper's direction.
- **Books:** each day t within U_J:
  - long the top 10% of a score (round(0.1 × n)) and short the bottom 10%, equal weight;
  - bought at the close of t + 1 and sold at the close of t + 2, $1 a side;
  - net of 5 bps a side on turnover, Σ|Δw| against the previous day's book as it drifted.
- **The statistic:**
  - D(t) = book(P_full) − book(P_base), gross, daily, over decision days 2008-01-02..2019-12-31;
  - net D is the same with each book's costs.
- **Pass:** mean gross D > 0 with t ≥ 2 at the Holm bar below, **and** mean net D > 0.
- **The part beyond reversal:** both books use R(t) and V(t) with the same group-specific fit, and only the V·R term differs. If the fitted ĉ_g are near zero, the two books nearly coincide and D is about 0, a fail, as it should be.

### Statistics and the bar

- **R1's bootstrap:**
  - each stock-year's sums (Σxx′ and Σxy over its pairs) are kept per 21-trading-day block of the year (12 blocks; a short last block joins the one before);
  - a draw resamples each year's blocks with replacement, using the same draw for every stock, so stocks stay as correlated as they were;
  - each stock's OLS, the winsorized means and R1 are recomputed;
  - a stock-year with fewer than 100 pairs in a draw is left out of that draw.
- **M1's bootstrap:** circular blocks of 21 trading days of the daily D series.
- **Both:**
  - 10,000 draws, seed 20261025;
  - t = estimate ÷ bootstrap sd, and p = the one-sided normal p of that t. The bootstrap percentile p is reported beside it.
- **Reported beside them:** a Newey–West t for M1 (5 lags), and R1's plain t over its 12 yearly Δ's (11 degrees of freedom).
- **Holm across R1 and M1** at a one-sided family level of 0.0228: the smaller p must be below 0.0114 and the other below 0.0228. Each also needs t ≥ 2.
  - This is the bar `experiments/README.md` sets for published strategies, since the paper is the prior. Whether each primary also clears t ≥ 3 (p < 0.00135) is reported.
- **No grid:** each primary is a single statistic, so Romano–Wolf isn't needed.
  - The reported items below aren't candidates.
  - A variant that passes when its primary fails isn't a pass. It could only become the primary of a new test on forward data.
- **Every comparison** reports the estimate with a 90% interval, not only pass or fail.

## What the results can and can't show

- **R1 passes:** the paper's cross-sectional pattern holds on surviving US stocks in 2008–2019, with dollar volume as the proxy.
  - By the paper's own numbers, read it as "heavy-volume moves in thinly traded stocks reverse less", not as continuation, unless item 2 shows C1 + C2·V > 0 at common values of V.
  - It can't separate informed trading from bid-ask bounce or earnings drift (items 4 and 6 bear on both).
- **R1 fails with the wrong sign:** heavy volume marks more reversal in thin stocks too, as Conrad et al. found weekly. Survivors push this way, so it isn't a clean rejection.
- **R1 fails near zero:** no pattern in this period's survivors.
- **M1 passes:** the volume × return term improves a reversal book traded a day late, beyond R and V. It must then hold forward and on survivor-free data before it counts.
- **R1 passes and M1 fails:** the pattern is real on the next day, but not usable by a trader who needs the close's volume, or too small once reversal and costs are taken out.
- **Either way,** dollar volume is a crude proxy for information asymmetry. Nothing here tests the paper's model directly.

## Ways it can fool us

- **Reversal in disguise.** V·R carries R, and heavy-volume days have big moves. M1 compares against a base with R and V fitted the same way per third, and R1 has a C1 per stock.
- **Size-dependent reversal.** Reversal is stronger in small, illiquid stocks (Avramov et al.). M1 gives each third its own reversal coefficient; R1 has one per stock.
- **Bid-ask bounce.** These are closes, not mid-quotes.
  - Bounce makes C1 more negative in thin stocks. If heavy volume shrinks the bounce's share of a move, it also makes C2 positive: the claim's direction.
  - The $5 floor limits it, and the $1 floor is reported. A pass in R1 can't rule bounce out; the paper's mid-quote check could.
- **Earnings drift.** Spike days are mostly announcements, and drift after them looks like C2 > 0. Item 4 drops them.
- **Liquidity or information.** Dollar volume measures both. The paper argues liquidity predicts the opposite sign.
- **Dollar volume's price term.** Share volume is used instead.
- **Survivor-only stocks.** The price data holds only companies still listed at the export's end.
  - **R1, against the claim:**
    - small stocks suffer most. A small company whose heavy-volume fall kept going to delisting is missing, and the survivors are those whose falls reversed. That pushes the low third's C2 down on down days;
    - companies taken over are missing too, with their run-ups on heavy volume before a bid: the paper's informed continuation.
  - **R1, for the claim:** companies that grew from small to large are over-represented, and their heavy-volume rises continued. That pushes C2 up on up days.
  - Hence the up/down split (item 3) and the results by year. The conditioning is longest in 2008, 17 years before the export ends.
  - **M1:** the paired books are drawn from the same survivors. But buying losers is flattered (`+18.6-monthly-top10-picks.md`), and the long-only judge account more so.
  - The fix is survivor-free prices (Sharadar; the code is ready in `stockai/docs/sharadar.md`).
- **Dividends after 2022.** Raw closes drop on ex-dates, often on heavier volume. That makes spurious heavy-volume down days in high-yield stocks, mostly large ones, in the check period and the unseen months. There are no dividend dates in hand, so it is noted, not fixed.
- **Broken volumes.** The new store's prints around 2026-03-03 would read as huge negative V. The 1% rule drops them.
- **Heavy tails.** Daily returns of thin stocks have huge outliers that steer OLS.
  - |R| > 0.5 is dropped and V is clipped;
  - C2 is winsorized in R1, and the regressors in M1.
- **Years already read.**
  - 2008–2019 has been read by every experiment.
  - Formula mining trained on 2005–2014 with return × volume-ratio products.
  - Twin reversal, close location and silent-peer drift touched volume and reversal.
- **A short first fit.** M1's first fits rest on about 480 origins from 2006–2007, because of the export's thin early years.

## Results (run 2026-10-25)

**What this tests.** Two things:
- **R1:** whether the paper's pattern holds on surviving US stocks after 2008, with dollar volume in place of size and spreads. The pattern is that heavy-volume moves reverse less, or continue, in thinly traded stocks.
- **M1:** whether a trader who learns the day's volume only at the close, and so trades at the next one, gains anything from it over plain reversal.

Both are judged on 2008–2019 under the README's bar for published strategies: Holm at a one-sided 0.0228, each needing t ≥ 2. R1's bootstrap t is also divided by 1.11, the spread of its shuffled-V null (Decisions).

**The tests, in order:**
1. **R1 (primary): fails.** The lowest third's C2 beat the highest's by +0.0057 (90% −0.0088..+0.0170).
   - Bootstrap t +0.72, or +0.65 scaled for the null; p 0.26.
   - Positive in 7 of 12 years: 2008–2011 yes, then mostly no.
   - The slope of C2 on the dollar-volume rank (the paper's equation 12, with dollar volume for size) has the right sign, −0.0066, but t −0.55.
2. **M1 (primary): fails.** Adding V·R to a reversal-and-volume book lost 1.49 bps a day gross (t −1.49) and 1.59 net. Positive in 5 of 12 years.
3. **The check period, 2020–2025-06:** neither is consistent. R1 is +0.0094 (t 0.74, p 0.23); M1 is −0.15 bps a day (t −0.12).
4. **Reported: the pattern is there across stocks on the next day, and gone the day after.**
   - In daily cross-sections of U_W, the coefficient on G·V·R for R(t + 1) is −0.044 (t −6.9; 2020–2025-06 t −3.1). The paper's direction: V·R predicts +0.028 for the lowest dollar volume and −0.017 for the highest.
   - For R(t + 2), the first return a next-close trader earns, it is +0.006 (t 1.1).
5. **The money:** traded daily, the 20-stock account made −12.0% a year at 5 bps against SPY's +11.4%. That is −23.4 points a year after costs, and −0.9 points a year against random before costs.
