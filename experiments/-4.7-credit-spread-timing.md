# Credit-spread timing: does a widening Baa spread warn stocks a month ahead?

**TL;DR:** Does a week of widening in the Baa corporate bond spread, while stocks haven't fallen, warn that stocks will fall over the next month? No: over 2008–2019 the week's spread change made out-of-sample forecasts of SPY's next 21 days 1.7% worse than forecasts from SPY's volatility, VIX and SPY's recent return alone (Clark-West t −1.52; the bar was +3), and a wider spread came before slightly *higher* returns, not lower ones. It runs the other way: a rise in SPY is followed by a narrowing spread over the next week (t −4.3). A rule that holds SPY by its own volatility but goes to cash on the claim's warning did no better than the same rule without the warning (Sharpe 0.73 against 0.79), and trailed SPY by 4.7 points a year after costs. Verdict: no edge; stocks lead the spread.

## The claim

As listed on the page:
- **The claim:** "When the Baa corporate bond spread over Treasuries widens over a week while stocks haven't fallen, stocks fall over the following month."
- **The reason given:** the bond market may price credit risk before the stock market does.

What it needs to be true, in this project's terms:
- **The spread leads:** a change in the spread carries information about SPY's next month that SPY's own price, its volatility and VIX don't carry yet.
- **The direction:** a wider spread comes before a lower return. The sign is part of the claim.
- **"While stocks haven't fallen":** the warning is the spread moving without the stock market. If stocks fell the same week, the stock market already has the news.

## Published record (novelty and background check, 2026-10-25)

Web searches made while writing this plan, before any data was computed. Only what was read is cited, with where it was read.

**Credit spreads and later stock prices:**
- **Gilchrist & Zakrajšek 2012**, *AER* 102(4): 1692–1720 (read: the NBER working paper w17021's abstract). They build a credit spread index from the prices of individual corporate bonds and split it into expected defaults and a residual, the excess bond premium. Innovations in the excess bond premium "are shown to lead to significant declines in economic activity and equity prices." This is a monthly macro model, not a trading rule, and the premium needs their bond-level data. FRED's Moody's index isn't that index.
- **Welch & Goyal 2008**, *Review of Financial Studies* 21(4): 1455–1508 (read: the NBER working paper 10483, 2004). Their predictors include the default yield spread (Baa minus Aaa) and the default return spread (long-term corporate minus government bond returns). Out of sample, each "had pretty consistently zero or negative performance."
- **Fama & French 1989**, *Journal of Financial Economics* 25: 23–49 (read only through abstracts and later papers' summaries, not the paper). The *level* of the default spread is one of the business-conditions proxies that predict stock and bond returns at long horizons, with *higher* expected returns when spreads are high. That is a risk premium, the opposite sign to the claim's, and about levels, not weekly changes.

**Which market moves first:**
- **Kwan 1996**, *JFE* 40(1): 63–80 (abstract): lagged stock returns explain current changes in the same firm's bond yields, and lagged bond yield changes don't explain stock returns. Stocks lead.
- **Gebhardt, Hvidkjaer & Swaminathan 2005**, *JFE* 75: 651–690 (abstract, through a summary page): a firm's past stock returns predict its bonds' returns. Momentum spills over from stocks to bonds.
- **Downing, Underwood & Xing 2009**, *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis* 44(5): 1081–1102 (abstract): hourly stock returns lead bond returns for non-convertible junk and BBB bonds. The corporate bond market is less informationally efficient than the stock market.
- **Hotchkiss & Ronen 2002**, *RFS* 15(5): 1325–1354 (abstract): for high-yield bonds, stocks don't lead bonds; neither market leads.
- **Norden & Weber 2009**, *European Financial Management* 15(3): 529–562 (abstract): over 2000–2002, stock returns lead changes in credit default swap and bond spreads.
- **Acharya & Johnson 2007**, *JFE* 84: 110–141 (abstract): the credit default swap market reveals information beyond what stocks show, but only for bad credit news, for firms that later suffer, and more for firms with many relationship banks. It is evidence of informed trading in single-name credit swaps, not a forecast of the stock market.

**What wasn't found:** a published test of the claim as stated, a weekly change in an aggregate Baa spread, conditioned on stocks not having fallen, forecasting the index's next month. It shows up in practitioner commentary without a formal test. So the published record doesn't supply a prior for it. Where it speaks to direction, it mostly says stocks lead bonds, with credit default swaps as the exception for bad news at single firms.

**Why the bar is t ≥ 3:** the exact rule isn't a published result, so it is graded as a new idea, not at the published-strategy bar of t ≥ 2.

## What the project already measured

- **Timing SPY by its own volatility works.** In `-3.2-market-timing.md`, volatility-managed SPY had a Sharpe ratio of 0.70 over 2005–2025 against 0.58 for buy-and-hold. A credit signal has to add to that.
- **VIX and SPY's own realized variance are already in hand** as forecasters of SPY's next month (the baseline of an earlier volatility-timing test, not yet published here). A widening spread usually comes with rising volatility, so the test must separate the two.
- **The credit series were among the 1,753 outside series** tested as leaders of individual stocks (`+4.0-outside-data-leaders.md`). Nothing led: strong lagged links appeared 1.04 times as often as chance, the same as pure noise, and every category was between 0.81 and 1.10 times chance. The same series as model inputs added nothing once revised data was set aside (`+20.9-outside-data-inputs-1-week.md` and the one- and two-month files). Those tests used the census's stock-by-stock machinery. None asked this plan's question: SPY over a fixed 21 days, with volatility and VIX as controls.
- **The catalog's timing probe** (read while checking publication timing; it was computed for the catalog on 2005–2025, before this plan). A day's change in BAA10Y correlates −0.22 with SPY's same-day return, −0.12 with SPY's previous day, and −0.37 with the same day's change in the 10-year Treasury yield. Its look-ahead partial against SPY's next day is +0.01. These are same-day and one-day relations, not this plan's 21-day test. Two things follow for the design. Part of the spread's move follows yesterday's stock market (the reverse direction, check C1). And part of it is the Treasury leg (checks C2 and C4).

## The data

**The spread.** FRED's BAA10Y is Moody's seasoned Baa corporate bond yield (DBAA) minus the 10-year Treasury constant-maturity yield (DGS10), in percentage points. FRED's notes:
- Moody's averages are "based on bonds with maturities 20 years and above."
- Treasury data has come directly from the Treasury since 2019-06-21.

So the spread compares bonds of 20 years and more with a 10-year Treasury. When Treasury yields fall in a flight to safety, BAA10Y widens even if corporate yields don't move. AAA10Y is the same for Moody's Aaa. BAA10Y − AAA10Y = DBAA − DAAA, the default spread, has no Treasury leg. Moody's Aaa average has used industrial bonds only since 2001-12-07 (footnote 14 of the Fed's H.15 release of 2015-03-02).

**Checked (no values computed):**
- BAA10Y and AAA10Y have 9,197 rows each, 1990-01-02..2026-10-15, no missing-value markers. VIXCLS has 9,296 rows and DGS10 9,203, over the same dates.
- On stock trading days, BAA10Y has no value on 48 export days (2001-12-31..2026-10-15) and 69 Yahoo SPY days (1993-01-29..2026-10-15). They are the bond-market holidays when stocks trade: Columbus Day, Veterans Day, and Martin Luther King Day before 1998. Never two in a row.
- VIXCLS misses 2 Yahoo SPY days (1997-01-31 and 1997-11-26) and no export day.
- Yahoo SPY: 8,489 days, 1993-01-29..2026-10-20.
- Store: SPY, XLF and the other SPDR sector funds 2005-02-25..2025-06-27 (old store, 5,116 rows) and ..2026-10-19 (new store, 5,445 rows). LQD and IEF from 2005-02-25, HYG from 2007-04-11.
- Exports: old 2001-12-31..2025-06-27 (5,911 days), new to 2026-10-19 (6,240).

### When BAA10Y for day t is public

**What was found (2026-10-25):**
- **FRED today.** DBAA for 2026-10-22 was posted 2026-10-23 at 10:31 AM CDT. A search result shows the 2026-04-07 value posted 2026-04-08 at 10:19 AM CDT. BAA10Y, FRED's computed spread, for 2026-10-22 was posted 2026-10-23 at 4:03 PM CDT (5:03 PM New York), after that day's stock close. FRED shows only the latest posting time. ALFRED's page for DBAA lists no vintages, so earlier posting times couldn't be checked.
- **The Fed's H.15 release, one example checked.** H.15 carried Moody's seasoned Aaa and Baa yields. Its release of Monday 2015-03-02 holds values through Friday 2015-02-27, the previous business day. It says the weekly release is posted Mondays and daily updates Tuesday to Friday, and the Fed's current calendar lists H.15 at 4:15 PM. If that schedule held then, day t's Moody's yield was public at about 4:15 PM New York on the next business day. Only this one release was checked, so the schedule in other years is an inference.
- **DGS10 for day t** is the Treasury's ~15:30 quote on day t itself (catalog `known_after_days` 0).
- **The catalog** gives Moody's spreads `known_after_days` 1: "published the next morning / end-of-day prices."

**The rule (conservative, and the same treatment the earlier volatility-timing test gave VIX):** the state computed after the close of decision day t uses the spread only through the previous trading day, t − 1. The trade is at the close of t + 1.
- Under both schedules above, day t − 1's value was public by about 4:15–5:05 PM New York on day t. That is after day t's 16:00 stock close, but before a decision made after that close for a trade at the next close.
- Day t's own spread wasn't public by the close of t + 1 under H.15's 4:15 PM schedule, so it isn't used.
- On a bond holiday the spread carries the previous value.

**Reported, not pass/fail (C5):**
- **Optimistic timing:** the spread through t, traded at the close of t + 1. It is feasible today, when FRED posts DBAA in the late morning of t + 1, but not under H.15's 4:15 PM schedule.
- **Extra caution:** the spread through t − 2.

## Rules (proposed; fixed before any result is seen)

### Timeline, symbols and leakage

- **Days** are trading days. P1 and the checks use Yahoo SPY's trading days, which run back to 1993. S2 uses the exports' days (`-3.2`'s calendar).
- **Decision day t.** Everything is computed after the close of t. Any trade is at the close of t + 1.
- **Prices.** P is Yahoo's adjusted close for SPY: split- and dividend-adjusted, so a total-return index, and the same series the after-costs number uses. It is used for every SPY quantity in P1, S1, S2 and the checks. The store's SPY is dividend-adjusted only through about 2022-12 and raw after that. It is used only in a reported repeat of S2. Daily log return: r(t) = log P(t) − log P(t − 1).
- **The spread on a day w:** S(w), FRED's BAA10Y dated w, or the latest earlier value on a bond holiday.
- **The week's spread change:** ΔS(t) = S(t − 1) − S(t − 6), the last five daily changes that are public at t (in percentage points; 0.01 is 1 bp).
- **SPY's return since that week began:** R(t) = log P(t) − log P(t − 6). It runs from the close where the spread's week starts to the latest close. So "stocks haven't fallen" uses all the stock prices public at t.
- **Realized variance:** RV_k(t) is the sum of max(r², 10⁻⁸) over the k days ending at t, for k = 1, 5 and 22 (the HAR terms of the earlier volatility-timing test).
- **VIX:** V(t) is FRED's VIXCLS for day t, set at 16:15. It is allowed because the trade is at t + 1, as in the earlier volatility-timing test. On its two missing days it carries the previous day's value.
- **Target:** y(t) = log P(t + 22) − log P(t + 1), SPY's log total return over the 21 days from the trade's close. It is the raw return, since cash earns 0 throughout.
- **Training eligibility:** a fit made at day t may use origin s only if its whole target has been observed: s + 1 + h ≤ t, which is s + 22 ≤ t for h = 21. The same rule covers every estimated quantity. The code asserts it for every fit.
- **Old span and unseen months:** in the old-span runs every series is cut at the old export's last day, 2025-06-27. No target may end later. Yahoo's file and FRED's files run into the unseen months, so the cut is made in code and asserted.

### P1 (primary). Does the week's spread change add to what already forecasts SPY?

- **Baseline model B:** least squares with an intercept on log RV₁, log RV₅, log RV₂₂, log V and R. These are SPY's own variance, VIX and SPY's recent move: what already works, plus the control the claim names.
  - The 500-stock dispersion and correlation terms of the earlier volatility-timing test are left out. They are built from survivor-only stocks, and leaving them out keeps every series in this test survivor-free.
- **Spread model:** B plus ΔS.
- **Fitting:**
  - **Expanding window,** from the first origin with every input (about 1993-03).
  - **Refits** every 21 trading days on `-3.2`'s calendar (export days 252 + 21k), extended back in steps of 21 trading days before 2003.
  - **Forecasts:** each fit forecasts the origins up to the next refit day.
  - **Both models are fitted on the same origins.**
  - **A fit needs at least 1,260 eligible origins,** so forecasts start in about 1998.
- **Periods, by origin date:**
  - early, 1998–2007: reported only;
  - development, 2008-01-02..2019-12-31: the pass test;
  - check, 2020-01-02 to the last origin whose target ends by 2025-06-27;
  - the unseen months, once, at the end.
- **Clark-West statistic,** as in the earlier volatility-timing test:
  - the per-origin statistic f = e_B² − e_S² + (ŷ_B − ŷ_S)², where e is a model's error and ŷ its forecast;
  - its mean's t-statistic with Newey-West standard errors at 42 lags (2h, because the 21-day targets overlap);
  - a moving-block bootstrap of the centred f, 63-day blocks, 10,000 draws, with the forecasts fixed. The one-sided p is the share of resampled means at or above the observed mean.
  - **Why 63-day blocks rather than the brief's 21:** f inherits the 21-day overlap of the targets plus the clustering of volatility, so a 21-day block cuts through the dependence. The 21-day block p is reported next to it.
- **Pass (development):** all four of
  1. Clark-West t ≥ 3;
  2. bootstrap p < 0.0013;
  3. a lower raw mean squared error than B. Clark-West alone can be significant while the larger model's forecasts are worse;
  4. the claimed sign: the median of the development fits' coefficients on ΔS is negative. A significant positive coefficient would say a widening comes before *higher* returns. That is a result, but not the claim.
- **Check (2020–2025-06):** consistent if the raw error is lower, Clark-West's one-sided normal p is below 0.05 and the median coefficient is negative. It is not an independent confirmation: `-3.2`, the earlier volatility-timing test and others read these years for SPY timing.
- **Reported with P1:**
  - each model's out-of-sample R² against the expanding historical mean of y, the yardstick of Welch & Goyal;
  - the error ratio 1 − MSE_S ÷ MSE_B with a 95% bootstrap interval;
  - all of it by year, for the early period, and without 2008–2009 and without 2020;
  - the fitted coefficient on ΔS over time.
- **Code checks, run before P1's result is read:**
  - **noise:** 200 noise series built like ΔS (iid daily changes, summed over five days) and 200 with the daily spread changes' first-order autocorrelation go through the whole P1 pipeline. They show how often noise reaches t ≥ 3 and how often it lowers the error;
  - **a leaky oracle:** the future target plus noise, which must win by far.

### S1 (secondary). The claim's conditional form

- **Model:** B plus ΔS · 1[R ≥ 0]. The spread's widening enters only in weeks when SPY hadn't fallen.
- **The same statistics as P1.**
- **Bar (development):** Clark-West one-sided p < 0.05 by both the Newey-West t and the 63-day bootstrap, a lower raw error than B, and a negative median coefficient.
- **It counts only if P1 passes.** If S1 passes and P1 fails, that isn't a pass: S1 would become the primary of a new test, judged on forward data only.
- **Reported:** whether the interaction adds to P1's spread model (B + ΔS against B + ΔS + ΔS · 1[R ≥ 0]).
- **Why the conditional form isn't the primary:** the condition throws away the weeks when stocks fell, about half of them, and its cut-off is a free choice. A continuous change with SPY's own return as a control asks the same question, "the spread moved and stocks didn't", without a threshold.

### S2 (secondary). A SPY-or-cash rule against variance timing

`-3.2`'s set-up, with the earlier volatility-timing test's timing:
- **Calendar.** Decisions on `-3.2`'s fixed calendar (export days 252 + 21k), using the old export for the old span. Each decision is made after the close of t0, bought at the close of t0 + 1 and held to the close of t0 + 22.
- **Accounts.** SPY or cash at 0, bought and held within the month. 5 bps is charged on every SPY trade: the first buy, each month's change, and the trim back to target from the drifted weight.
- **Prices.** `engine.py`'s functions (`rel_paths`, `daily_returns`, `measures`, `random_timing`, `block_bootstrap_sharpe`) are imported unchanged and given Yahoo SPY on export days.

**The rules:**
- **Baseline rule B (variance timing, point in time):** w_B = min(1, c ÷ RV(t0)).
  - RV is `-3.2`'s quantity: the variance (ddof 0) of SPY's 21 daily log returns ending at t0.
  - c is the median of that same quantity over the previous 1,260 trading days (at least 252), not counting t0. So c is known at t0, unlike `-3.2`'s exposure-matched c.
- **The spread rule:** w_S = 0 for the month if the claim's warning fires at t0, otherwise w_B.
- **The warning fires** when both hold:
  - ΔS(t0) is above 0 and at or above the 80th percentile of ΔS over the previous 1,260 trading days (at least 252; numpy's linear interpolation): a widening in the top fifth of recent weeks;
  - R(t0) ≥ 0: stocks haven't fallen since the spread's week began.

**Bar (decisions 2008-01..2019-12):** a higher Sharpe ratio at 5 bps than rule B, with a paired circular block bootstrap of months p < 0.10. The bootstrap uses `-3.2`'s blocks of 12 months and 10,000 draws. This is `-3.2`'s test 2, and the earlier volatility-timing test's J2a bar. It counts only if P1 passes.
- **Why 12-month blocks rather than the brief's 21 days:** exposures are set once a month, so the unit is the month, and volatility clusters across months. The same block as `-3.2` also keeps the p comparable with its table. The p with 1-month (21-day) blocks is reported beside it.
- **Check (2020-01..2025-06):** the effect size with its interval, by period.

**The README numbers come from the spread rule:**
- **Before costs, vs random:** its compound annual return minus the median of 10,000 random reorderings of its own monthly exposures, decisions 2008-01..2025-06. Rule B's is reported beside it.
- **After costs:** its yearly return at 5 bps a side minus SPY's total return (Yahoo), over calendar years 2008..2025 by trading day: 2008 from the first trade's close, 2025 to the last month's end. It is copied from `research/after_costs/group_b/market_timing.py`, shifted to the close of t0 + 1 as in the earlier volatility-timing test. Rule B's is reported beside it.

**Reported with S2:**
- buy-and-hold;
- each rule's annual return, Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown, exposure, switches and costs;
- the share of decisions where the warning fires;
- results by period: 2008–2009, 2010–2014, 2015–2019 and 2020–2025-06;
- the same rules on the store's SPY.

**Threshold grid, reported only:** the widening threshold in {above 0, top fifth (the rule), top tenth} × the stock condition in {R ≥ 0 (the rule), none}, six rules. Each is set against rule B, with Romano-Wolf max-t adjusted p-values from the same paired bootstrap draws. A grid rule that passes when the main rule fails isn't a pass.

**Its limit:** the calendar reads the warning on one day in 21. Most days when it fires fall between decision dates. P1's daily origins are where the full signal is read.

### Checks (reported, not pass/fail)

Each check repeats P1's method, unless it says otherwise, with the change named.
- **C1. The reverse direction: do stocks lead the spread?** Two regressions on daily origins with same-dated data. This check describes which market moves first and doesn't trade, so it doesn't need the publication lag.
  - (a) S(t + 5) − S(t) on R₅(t) = log P(t) − log P(t − 5) and S(t) − S(t − 5);
  - (b) log P(t + 5) − log P(t) on the same two regressors;
  - Newey-West at 10 lags. Also the same at 21 days (42 lags).
  - Reported for 1993–2007, 2008–2019 and 2020–2025-06. The published record says stocks lead (Kwan 1996; Norden & Weber 2009; Downing et al. 2009).
- **C2. The default spread:** BAA10Y − AAA10Y in place of BAA10Y, in P1, S1 and S2, with the same timing. Both are Moody's 20-year-plus averages, so the Treasury leg and its maturity mismatch drop out. Moody's Aaa average is industrials only.
- **C3. Financials:** the sector most exposed to credit.
  - P1 with XLF's next-21-day return as the target and XLF's own RV₁, RV₅, RV₂₂ and R in the baseline, VIX as is. XLF is from the store, so these fits need only 504 eligible origins (the store starts 2005-02-25).
  - P1 with XLF − SPY as the target, keeping SPY's baseline terms.
  - S2's rules on XLF: XLF's own variance timing, and the same with the warning.
  - The store's XLF is dividend-adjusted only through about 2022-12, so its 2023+ returns miss XLF's dividends (roughly 0.1–0.2% a month, judging from its usual yield; not measured). That is a level shift, mostly absorbed by the intercept.
- **C4. The Treasury leg:** the week's change in DGS10, DGS10(t − 1) − DGS10(t − 6), added to both models.
- **C5. Timing:** the optimistic version (the spread through t) and the extra-cautious one (through t − 2), described above.
- **C6. The spread's level:** S(t − 1) added to both models (Fama & French's risk premium; Welch & Goyal's dfy is the Baa − Aaa level).
- **C7. Horizons:** h = 5 and h = 63, with Newey-West at 2h lags and bootstrap blocks of max(63, 3h) days.
- **C8. A traded credit spread with no publication lag:** −(LQD's 5-day log return − IEF's) through the close of t, from Yahoo (from 2002-07-30), in place of ΔS. LQD holds investment-grade corporate bonds and IEF 7–10-year Treasuries; both close at 16:00. It shows whether a bond-market signal that is public at once does better than Moody's index.
- **C9. The early period, 1998–2007:** P1's own forecasts before 2008, from the same expanding window.
- **C10. Crises:** P1 and S2 without 2008–2009 and without 2020, and by year.

A check or variant that passes when P1 fails isn't a pass. It would become the primary of a new test, judged on forward data only.

### The bar, in one place

- **P1 is the only primary,** so there is no Holm correction: t ≥ 3, and the one-sided bootstrap p < 0.0013.
- **S1 and S2 are secondary.** They are judged at their own bars only if P1 passes.
- **The grid in S2** gets Romano-Wolf p-values. Everything else is reported.

## What the results can and can't show

- **P1 passes and holds up:** the week's change in Moody's Baa spread carries information about SPY's next month beyond SPY's volatility, VIX and its recent return, out of sample, in the claimed direction. That supports "bonds price credit risk first" at the index level, at the resolution of a daily average of seasoned long bonds. C2 and C4 then say whether it is credit or the Treasury leg, and C1 whether the spread also follows stocks.
- **P1 fails:** it rejects this reading of the claim (Moody's index, a five-day change, 21 days, 2008–2019). It doesn't reject credit information in general: single-name credit swaps, firm-level bonds, the excess bond premium at monthly horizons, or spread levels over years.
- **A positive coefficient:** a widening followed by higher returns would fit a risk premium (Fama & French 1989), not the claim.
- **S2 passes when P1 does:** the information is big enough, and arrives often enough on the calendar, to improve on variance timing after costs.

## Ways it can fool us

- **Volatility in disguise.** Spreads widen when the market is volatile, and volatility predicts both the size of moves and risk-managed returns. Hence RV and VIX in the baseline, and B as S2's yardstick.
- **The Treasury leg.** BAA10Y moves against the 10-year yield. A flight to Treasuries widens it with no news about credit, so a "warning" could be a bond rally. C2 and C4 separate them.
- **A slow index.** Moody's averages are seasoned long bonds, and the published record says bond prices follow stocks with a lag. A spread that widens "while stocks haven't fallen" can be the index catching up with an earlier fall, which predicts nothing. C1 measures it, and C8 uses a traded spread with no lag.
- **Publication timing.** A day too early would leak. The rule uses the spread through t − 1 for a trade at t + 1. C5 shows what one day either way changes.
- **Overlapping targets.** Daily 21-day targets overlap, so plain t-statistics are inflated. Hence Newey-West, the block bootstrap and the eligibility rule.
- **A few crises.** Credit stress in these years comes in a few episodes, 2008–2009 and 2020 above all. Results are reported by year and without 2008–2009 and 2020.
- **Few warnings on the calendar.** S2 has about 144 development months, and the warning may fire in a dozen or two of them. That is why P1, with daily origins, is the primary.
- **Revised data.** FRED serves its current vintage, and earlier vintages couldn't be checked. Whether Moody's daily yields were ever revised is unknown. They are market yields, not estimates re-made later like the series that flattered `+20.9-outside-data-inputs-1-week.md`, so the risk looks small, but it isn't verified.
- **Choosing after looking.** Every model, threshold, window and check is fixed here. The catalog's same-day and one-day correlations (above) were seen before the plan was written. They show nothing at 21 days, and they are why C1, C2 and C4 exist.
- **The survivor-only stock data** doesn't enter: every series here is a fund (SPY, XLF, LQD, IEF), an index (VIX) or a FRED series. The exports supply only the trading calendar.
- **The store's prices** enter only through XLF (C3) and the repeat of S2 on the store's SPY. Their dividends are raw after about 2022-12. SPY is Yahoo's total return everywhere else.

## Results (run 2026-10-25)

**What this tests.** It tests the claim as P1 reads it: the five-day change in FRED's Moody's Baa spread over the 10-year Treasury, public through t − 1, as a forecast of SPY's next 21 trading days. The test is out of sample, against SPY's own variance, VIX and SPY's recent return. S2 tests a SPY-or-cash rule that acts on the claim's warning.

**The tests, in order:**
1. **P1 (primary): fails.** Over 2008–2019 the spread model's error was 1.68% *higher* than the baseline's (95% −3.00%..+0.04%). Clark-West t was −1.52 and the bootstrap p 0.90. The median coefficient was positive (+0.0075), so a widening came before slightly higher returns. The bar was t ≥ 3, p < 0.0013, a lower error and a negative coefficient; none of the four holds. 2020–2025-06 isn't consistent either: +0.11%, t 0.74.
2. **S1 (secondary, the conditional form): fails its own bar.** In 2008–2019: −0.04%, t 0.60, p 0.28, coefficient positive.
3. **S2 (secondary, the money rule): fails its own bar.** Over 2008–2019 the spread rule's Sharpe ratio was 0.729 against 0.791 for rule B, bootstrap p 0.93 (bar < 0.10). After costs it trailed SPY by 4.7 points a year (rule B by 3.4), and it was +0.0 over random reorderings of its own exposures.
4. **The checks (reported): stocks lead the spread.** A 5-day rise in SPY is followed by a narrower spread over the next 5 days (t −4.27 in 2008–2019, −3.44 in 2020–2025, −2.55 in 1993–2007). The spread's change doesn't predict SPY (t −1.43). No forecasting check adds to the baseline in 2008–2019.

### Files

Code in `research/credit_spread/` (numpy only, system `python3 -I -B`, each under `systemd-run --user --scope -p MemoryMax=5G` with 4 threads). Run in this order:
- `inputs.py old`: the daily inputs and their coverage → `inputs_old.txt`, `.json` (about 1 s).
- `forecast.py old --noise-calibration 200` → `check_noise_old.txt`, `.json` (about 20 s); then `forecast.py old`: P1, S1, C2–C10 → `p1_old.txt`, `.json` (seconds).
- `timing.py old`: S2 → `s2_old.txt`, `.json`, `months_old.tsv` (about 10 s).
- `leadlag.py old`: C1 → `c1_old.txt`, `.json`.
- `check_random_warnings.py old|new`: the diagnostic added after the results → `check_random_warnings_<tag>.txt`, `.json`.
- `unseen.py`: all of it on the new export, once → `inputs_new`, `p1_new`, `s2_new`, `months_new.tsv`, `c1_new`, `unseen.txt`, with logs.
- `forward.py EXPORT_DIR STORE_DIR FRED_DIR YAHOO_DIR`: each later download, through the frozen code.
- `cs_common.py`: the fixed rules as constants, the loaders, fitting and statistics.
- `first_run/`: the first run's outputs and hashes, before deviation 1.

Large files (`~/.local/share/correlation2/experiments/credit_spread/`): `inputs_<tag>.npz` (the daily inputs) and `fc_<tag>.npz` (P1's forecasts and fits).
