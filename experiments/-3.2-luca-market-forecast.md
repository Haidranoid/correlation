# Forecasting the alcohol market itself: which of Luca's theses beat the market's own history?

**TL;DR:** Luca's third idea: forecast the alcohol market itself (what people buy) instead of the stocks, testing his theses one by one. The target was next year's US spending on wine, beer, spirits and restaurant alcohol, adjusted for prices. His theses (the economy, prices, search trends, new markets such as cannabis, and age) forecast it *worse* than the market's own recent growth over 2008–2019 (out-of-sample R² −3.2%, Clark-West t +0.69; the bar was +3), and worse than 92% of models built from random noise. Each thesis alone was negative (−8% to −38%), the news added nothing, wine was the worst market (−35%), and the check period and unseen months agree. The best forecast was the simplest: "next year grows as fast as last year", off by 1.7–1.9 points of growth. The same model didn't carry over to liquor-store sales, TTB shipments or Massachusetts cannabis. Verdict: the market is forecastable from itself, not from these theses.

## The claim

Luca's messages, in his words:
- "you are still focusing on the stock market tho, but what about focusing on the market forecasts itself, and see if the forecast is good testing different thesis?"
- "That market data can be interesting for companies"
- From his first message: wine "consumptions are not influence much by speculations rather than by social trends, economy, age or opening of new markets like cannabis, hard seltzers, psychedelics", and "try to see if you can forecast those markets based on these factors + the news".
- The follow-up: a model "that eventually can be applied to multiple markets like alcohol, weed etc".

What it needs to be true, in this project's terms:
- **The market, not the stock.** The target is what people buy: monthly US spending on wine, beer and spirits, and alcohol bought with meals, adjusted for price changes. Nothing here is traded.
- **"The forecast is good" means beating the market's own history.** Sales growth persists: the earlier test's C4 found that the latest 12-month growth of liquor-store sales alone forecast its value three months later with R² +10% (2008–2019) and +64% (2020–2025). A model that only rediscovers that is no use to a company. So every thesis is scored against a forecast built from the market's own recent growth, and must add to it.
- **"Testing different thesis".** Each of Luca's factors becomes a thesis with its own inputs, scored alone (P2) and together (P1).
- **"Interesting for companies".** Budgets are set a year ahead, so the primary horizon is the next 12 months against the 12 months before. Three months ahead is reported (C2), and the errors are also given in percentage points of growth (C5).
- **"Multiple markets".** The same model, unchanged, is run on other measures of the alcohol market (C4) and on a cannabis market (C8).

## Published record (novelty and background check, 2026-10-26)

**Forecasting sales with searches and the economy**
- **Choi & Varian 2012**, *Economic Record* 88 (special issue), "Predicting the Present with Google Trends" (read: Google Research's blog post and summaries, not the paper). Search counts improve *nowcasts* (the current month, before official data arrive) of retail, auto and home sales; a summary puts the gain at 5–20% lower mean absolute error. Nowcasting is a different task from forecasting a year ahead.
- **Chen & Chen, "Google Trends and Product Market: Evidence from the Wine Market"** (AAEA annual meeting, 2017; read: the listing only, which has no abstract). Its results are unknown here.
- **Algieri, Iania, Leccadito & Meloni 2024**, *Journal of Wine Economics* 19: 64–91, "Message in a bottle: Forecasting wine prices" (read: the abstract). Consumer surveys and macroeconomic factors improved forecasts of fine-wine and alcohol *prices* at horizons from one month to two years. Prices, not quantities.
- **No published out-of-sample test** of forecasting US wine, beer or spirits *quantities* from the economy, searches, demographics and cannabis together against an own-history benchmark was found in a short search. Industry forecasters (IWSR, NielsenIQ, Statista) publish forecasts without accuracy records; Statista says it uses exponential trend smoothing with GDP and consumer spending as drivers.

**The theses**
- **Age: DellaVigna & Pollet 2007**, *American Economic Review* 97(5) (read for the earlier test). Cohort sizes make demand for age-sensitive goods, beer among them, forecastable years ahead. Their horizon is five to ten years.
- **New markets: cannabis.** A working paper using Nielsen scanner data for more than 2,000 counties, 2006–2015 (Baggio, Chong & Kwon; read: NORML's and Ganjapreneur's summaries) found monthly alcohol sales about 15% lower after *medical* cannabis laws (beer about −14%, wine about −16%). A study funded by the Distilled Spirits Council (2019; read: its press release) found per-capita spirits sales up 3.6–7.6% and beer down 2.3–3.6% in Colorado, Washington and Oregon after *recreational* legalization, in line with national trends, and wine mixed. The evidence conflicts, and one side is industry-funded.
- **Prices and the economy.** Alcohol demand falls when its price rises; meta-analyses put wine's price elasticity around −0.7 (Wagenaar et al. 2009, *Addiction*; from memory, not re-read). Whether drinking rises or falls in recessions is disputed in the health-economics literature (Ruhm; Dee; not re-read).

  - **B, beer** (`DMLTRA`), off-premises;
  - **S, spirits** (`DLIQRA`), off-premises;
  - **R, restaurants and bars** (`DAPMRA`): alcohol in purchased meals.

  - **Revised.** These are today's values, after all of BEA's revisions, and BEA keeps no public vintages of its underlying detail. BEA estimates monthly product detail partly from indicator series such as retail sales and benchmarks it to annual data later (its methods in general terms; not checked for these lines). So the own-history benchmark, the models and the outcomes all use revised data. The inputs aren't revised (below), so revisions can't carry future information into them, but the level of accuracy here is that of a forecaster with perfect past data.
- **Other measures of the market (C4):**

- **Cannabis (C8):** Massachusetts Cannabis Control Commission, adult-use retail sales by day and product category (`a_sales_au_gross.csv`, from 2018-11) and average price per gram (`a_sales_au_price_per_gram.csv`). Summed to calendar months. It's the only state whose monthly sales downloaded directly: Colorado's page sits behind a browser check, and Washington publishes yearly county files.
- **Data checks (coverage only, before any result):**

  - **Consumer Expenditure Survey:** alcohol spending for all households and the seven age groups, 1984–2024; the combined 65+ group starts in 1988 and isn't used.
  - **TTB:** wine and spirits 2012-01..2026-03, beer 2012-01..2026-06. Wine's taxable withdrawals include hard cider. The series used are the category totals: wine "Taxable Withdrawals", beer "Taxable" removals, spirits "Taxable" withdrawals.

## Rules (proposed; fixed before any result is seen)

### Months and timing

- **Origins.** A forecast is made at origin month L, when BEA publishes month L (about four weeks after L ends). Everything it uses must have been published by then.
- **Targets.** For a monthly series X and horizon h, the target is y_h(L) = log Σ_{j=1..h} X(L+j) − log Σ_{j=1..h} X(L+j−12): the next h months against the same months a year earlier. For h = 12 it is next year's growth.
- **Own history.** g_h(L) = log Σ_{j=0..h−1} X(L−j) − log Σ_{j=0..h−1} X(L−j−12): the latest h months against a year earlier. For h = 12 it is last year's growth.
- **Periods, by the target's last month L + h:**
  - development 2008-01..2019-12 (the pass test);
  - check 2020-01..2025-06;
  - the unseen months 2025-07..2026-08, once, at the end.

### The theses and their inputs (all at origin L; none revised)

- **Economy**
  - E1, unemployment: U(L) − U(L−12), U = UNRATENSA (not seasonally adjusted, so not revised by seasonal factors).
  - E2, consumer sentiment: log S(L) − log S(L−12), S = UMCSENT (the month's final value).
  - E3, the stock market: SPY's 12-month log total return, between the last trading days of L − 12 and L (Yahoo adjusted closes).
- **Prices**
  - P, the beverage's relative price: [log C_b(L) − log C_b(L−12)] − [log C(L) − log C(L−12)].
  - C_b is the CPI for the target's beverage (wine at home `CUUR0000SEFW03`, beer at home `SEFW01`, spirits at home `SEFW02`, alcohol away from home `SEFX`). C is CPI all items (CPIAUCNS). All are NSA, which BLS doesn't revise.
  - In C4 and C8 the price is the nearest one: wine for TTB wine, beer for TTB beer, spirits for TTB spirits, alcohol overall (`CUUR0000SAF116`) for liquor stores, and Massachusetts' price per gram for cannabis.
- **Social trends**
  - T1, searches for alcohol: the 12-month log change of the earlier test's alcohol query ("wine + beer + whiskey + vodka + tequila").
  - T2, searches for drinking less: the same for "non alcoholic + sober + mocktail + dry january".
- **New markets**
  - T3, searches for new markets: the same for "marijuana + cannabis + hard seltzer + psilocybin".
  - LG, legal cannabis: the 12-month change in the share of the US population living in states (and DC) that have legalized recreational cannabis.
    - Dates come from the earlier test's C5 list; a state counts from its decision date.
    - Population weights are each state's 2020 estimate in `NST-EST2025-ALLDATA.csv`.
    - South Dakota is left out, because its 2020 measure was struck down in court.
- **Age**
  - A, demographic demand: the projected growth, from this year to next, of Σ_a pop(a) · w(a).
    - **Population:** pop(a) is the US resident population at age a from the latest Census estimate published by L. A July estimate counts from June of the next year: year(L) − 1 if month(L) ≥ 7, else year(L) − 2. It is aged forward one year per year to this year and next, ignoring deaths and migration; ages 85+ stay one group.
    - **Spending weights:** w(a) is alcohol spending per household whose reference person is in a's age group (the Consumer Expenditure Survey's seven groups: under 25, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74, 75+), relative to all households, averaged over the survey years published by L. Year Y is published in September of Y + 1. Ages under 21 get zero weight; the under-25 group's weight applies to ages 21–24.
    - A changes once a year. It is the age thesis at the shortest horizon it could act on; DellaVigna & Pollet's horizon (five to ten years) can't be tested here.
- **Months never published:** the October 2025 CPI and household survey were never released. Where an input's month-L value can't be computed for that reason, the input takes its value at the latest month where it can.
- **Timing check:** at origin L, the employment report for L (first Friday of L + 1), CPI for L (mid L + 1), sentiment's final value (end of L), Google's complete month (early L + 1) and month-end prices are all out before BEA's release for L.

### The model (the same for every market)

- **Benchmark (own history):** y = a + b · g_h, fitted by least squares on the eligible origins.
- **One fit per input k:** y = a + b · g_h + c_k · x_k.
- **The model** is the average of the nine single-input forecasts (E1, E2, E3, P, T1, T2, T3, LG, A): an equal-weight combination (Rapach, Strauss & Zhou 2010).
- **A thesis's forecast** is the average of its own inputs' forecasts.
- **The kitchen sink,** one regression on g_h and all nine inputs, is reported.
- **Eligibility:** a fit at origin L may use origin s only if s + h ≤ L, so its target was published by L (asserted for every fit).
- **Sample:** the origins where y, g_h and all nine inputs exist. Google's 12-month changes begin in 2005-01, so the sample starts there. A fit needs at least 36 eligible origins, so 12-month forecasts start about 2008-12. Every model, the benchmark included, is fitted on the same origins.

### P1 (primary). Does the model beat own history, 12 months ahead, across the four alcohol markets?

- **Pooled Clark-West:** for market i at origin L, d_i(L) = e_b² − e_m² + (f_b − f_m)², where e is an error and f a forecast, b the benchmark and m the model. The statistic is the mean over origins of the average of d_i over the four markets.
  - Its t-statistic uses Newey-West standard errors with 12 lags, because consecutive 12-month targets share 11 months.
  - The bootstrap is a moving-block bootstrap of the centred series, with 24-month blocks and 10,000 draws. The one-sided p is the share of resampled means at or above the observed mean.
- **Pooled out-of-sample R²:** 1 − Σ e_m² / Σ e_b², summed over the four markets and the period's origins, with a 95% block-bootstrap interval.
- **Pass (development):** all three of
  - Clark-West t ≥ 3;
  - bootstrap p < 0.0013;
  - pooled R² > 0.
- **Check (2020-01..2025-06):** consistent if the pooled R² is positive and the normal p is below 0.05.
- **Reported with P1:** each market alone (wine first), by year, and without targets that include 2020.

### P2. Each thesis alone

- **The test:** the same pooled test for each of the five theses' forecasts (economy, prices, social trends, new markets, age) against the benchmark.
- **Pass (development):** Clark-West t ≥ 3, R² > 0, and a Holm-adjusted bootstrap p < 0.0013 across the five.
- **Check:** as for P1.

### Checks (reported, not pass/fail)

- **C1. Detail:**
  - each input alone, pooled and for wine, with Holm-adjusted p across the nine for information;
  - the kitchen sink;
  - the model against the prevailing mean, and the benchmark against the prevailing mean, to show how much own history does by itself.
- **C2. Three months ahead:** P1 and P2 with h = 3 (Newey-West 12 lags, 24-month blocks, as above).
- **C3. Dollars:** P1 on current-dollar spending, what a company's revenue line sees.
- **C4. Other measures of the market:** the model, unchanged, on liquor-store sales and on TTB's wine, beer and spirits shipments, at h = 12 and h = 3. TTB's data starts in 2012, so its forecasts start around 2016, and its results are given for whatever origins exist in each period.
- **C5. Errors in plain units:** the mean absolute error, in percentage points of 12-month growth, of the prevailing mean, of last year's growth repeated (no change), of the benchmark and of the model. Also how often each got the direction right (faster or slower growth than last year). By market and period.
- **C6. The news:** the earlier test's monthly alcohol-headline share (N1) and tone (N2) as two more inputs, on the origins they cover (to 2022-09). Its sources change mix in 2012, so this is reported only.
- **C7. Extra caution:** every input one month older.
- **C8. Another market: cannabis in Massachusetts.**
  - **Target:** monthly adult-use retail sales in dollars. The model is unchanged, with the inputs that exist for it: E1–E3, T3, and the price per gram as P.
  - **Settings:** h = 3 and h = 12; at least 24 eligible origins per fit, because the market opened in 2018-11; all periods together.
  - **Limits:** the stores were closed from 2020-03-24 to 2020-05-25, and one young state can't settle anything. This is a demonstration that the model carries over, reported only.
- **C9. Code checks, run before P1's result is read:**
  - **noise:** 200 sets of nine AR(1) series with coefficient 0.95 (as persistent as 12-month changes) go through P1 in place of the inputs, to show how often noise reaches t ≥ 3 and a positive R²;
  - **a leaky oracle:** the future target plus noise as large as the target itself, as one more input. It must reach t > 3 and pooled R² > 0.3 (the earlier test's experience: such an oracle wins clearly but needn't reach p < 0.0013);
  - **eligibility:** asserted for every fit.

### What would count

- **P1 passes:** Luca's theses together forecast the alcohol market a year ahead better than its own momentum. C4 and C8 show whether that carries over to other measures and markets, and C5 shows whether the gain is big enough to matter to a company.
- **P1 fails:** a year ahead, the public information named here adds nothing a company couldn't get from the market's own recent growth. P2 shows whether any single thesis did better than the combination.
- **C1 shows the benchmark far ahead of the prevailing mean, and P1 fails:** the market is forecastable, but only from itself.

## Decisions

All of these were made on 2026-10-26 after C9's first run and before any line of P1, P2 or the other checks was computed. `forecast.py` hadn't been run.

1. **The oracle's assertion in C9.**
   - **What happened:** the plan asked the 1× oracle's own forecast to reach t > 3 and pooled R² > 0.3. It reached R² +0.28 and t 2.54 (bootstrap p 0.0006). The 0.5× oracle, which explains more than half the benchmark's squared error (R² +0.54), reached only t 2.46 (bootstrap p 0.0002).
   - **Why:** with 121 overlapping monthly targets and Newey-West at 12 lags, the development period holds about ten independent years. At that size, no forecast can reach a t of 3.
   - **The change:** the assertion now checks only that the pipeline sees a leak. The 0.5× oracle must reach R² > 0.3 and the 1× oracle R² > 0.2, each with bootstrap p < 0.0013, against the plan's benchmark.
   - **Unchanged:** P1's and P2's bar. The write-up will say that its t part can't be met at this sample size.
   - **Reading "as one more input":** the plan's "as one more input" is read as the oracle's own single-input forecast. The ten-input combination with the oracle in it is reported beside it.
   - **Precedent:** the earlier alcohol test's Decision 1.
2. **Noise beat the plan's benchmark: a two-lag benchmark is added, reported only.**
   - **What happened:** in C9's first run, nine random AR(1) series in place of the inputs gave a median pooled development R² of +9.6% against own history. t ≥ 3 came up in 1.0% of 200 sets (nominal 0.13%) and t ≥ 2 in 12.5%.
   - **Why:** independent noise can only lower accuracy on average. So the noise must be harvesting information that the one-lag benchmark leaves out. The likely source is the latest fully observed residual at origin L, y(L − 12) − a − b·g(L − 12). Since y(L − 12) is g(L), a benchmark with g(L) but not g(L − 12) leaves an annual-lag pattern for any persistent series to pick up by chance.
   - **The check:** P1′ and P2′ repeat P1 and P2 with g_h(L) and g_h(L − 12) in the benchmark and in every single-input fit, so the models stay nested. C9 reruns the noise against both benchmarks and computes the bootstrap p for every noise set (the full bar's false-positive rate). It also covers noise combinations of 1, 2, 3 and 9 series, the sizes of the theses and the model.
   - **No tuning:** the two-lag benchmark is the only one added, whatever the noise does against it; no further lags are tried.
   - **Unchanged:** P1 and P2 as the plan defines them (one lag), and their bar.
3. **How P1 and P2 will be read (fixed now, before they are computed).**
   - **Percentiles:** each result's development R², t and bootstrap p are reported as percentiles among the 200 noise sets of the same size (9 inputs for the model; 1, 2 or 3 for a thesis) and the same benchmark.
   - **Distinguishable from noise:** a forecast counts as distinguishable from noise only if its development R² is above the noise's 95th percentile against both benchmarks.
   - **Inside the band:** a positive R² inside the noise band on either benchmark is read as "no better than noise", whatever the formal bar says.
4. **The two-lag benchmark didn't explain the noise result** (C9's second run, still before P1).
   - **The noise got stronger, not weaker.** Against the two-lag benchmark, nine noise series gained a median R² of +19.2% (95th percentile +32.9%), against +9.6% (95th +23.8%) for the plan's benchmark. The two-lag benchmark is itself worse: its development error is 3.23 points of growth, against 2.34 for one lag (the benchmark error printed with the oracles).
   - **So the annual-lag hypothesis is rejected,** and, as Decision 2 fixed, no other benchmark is tried.
   - **A likelier explanation (not tested):** growth rates drift over the years, and an expanding-window fit adapts slowly. A persistent random series fitted in-sample often trends, so it acts as an accidental trend term that corrects some of that lag.
   - **The bootstrap gate is lenient here.** Against the plan's benchmark, 33% of the nine-series noise sets reached bootstrap p < 0.0013. The full bar (with t ≥ 3) was met by 0.5% of them, and by none against the two-lag benchmark.
   - **Unchanged:** Decision 3's reading. To count as distinguishable from noise, the model must exceed R² +23.8% against the plan's benchmark and +32.9% against the two-lag one.
5. **TTB withheld months (after P1 was read, before C4 was).** TTB marks spirits taxable withdrawals for 2025-07..2026-03 as withheld (blank values). They are treated as missing. They touch only spirits forecasts whose windows reach those months, none of them in the development or check periods.
6. **What the unseen run scores.** The plan says only that the unseen months are scored "once, at the end". `unseen.py` scores P1, P1′, P2, P2′, each market and C5's errors from the forecasts `forecast.py` saved, without refitting. With 14 overlapping targets, its t-statistics mean nothing, so only R² and errors are reported.

### What it means

- **For Luca's question:** none of his theses (the economy, prices, social trends, new markets, age) or the news forecast the US alcohol market a year ahead better than the market's own history. Together they did worse, and worse than 92% of random-noise models. Wine, his market, was the worst of the four (−35%).
- **The market is forecastable, but from itself.** "Next year grows like last year" missed by under 2 points of growth in 2008–2019. Nothing here improved on it, and after 2020 the long-run average was better.
- **For a business:** the yardstick is "same growth as last year" (and, after shocks, the long-run average). These public data, combined this way, don't beat it.
  - **The obvious next test:** forecast the change from last year's growth, fixed in advance. It would be a new plan, not a rerun of this one.
  - **The competition:** the paid forecasters (IWSR, NielsenIQ, Headset) have scanner and depletion data that aren't public.
