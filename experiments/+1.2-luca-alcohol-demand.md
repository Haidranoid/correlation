# Less speculative markets: do the economy, social trends and the news forecast alcohol stocks?

**TL;DR:** Luca's idea: wine and alcohol companies are driven by the economy, social trends and new markets (cannabis, hard seltzer, psychedelics) rather than by speculation, so they should be forecastable from those and the news. They weren't. Over 2008–2019, six public monthly fundamentals (unemployment, consumer sentiment, the price of alcohol, and Google searches for alcohol, for drinking less and for cannabis and similar "new markets") forecast an alcohol basket's next month against SPY *worse* than its plain average (out-of-sample R² −3.1%, Clark-West t −0.94; the bar was +3). The alcohol stocks weren't less speculative either: 24% yearly volatility against SPY's 17%, and no more forecastable than SPY, consumer discretionary or the Nasdaq-100. Alcohol *sales* were forecastable (R² +10% to +64%), but the stocks already priced it. Cannabis legalizations moved nothing, and alcohol headlines added nothing. A basket-or-SPY rule beat SPY by 1.2 points a year after costs only because it held the basket 94% of the time. Always holding the basket did +4.3, mostly one small stock's 2009 rebound in a list picked with hindsight, and the rule's timing was 2.2 points a year worse than random. Verdict: no edge; the demand is forecastable, the stocks aren't.

## The claim

Luca works in the wine industry. His messages, in his words:
- "we are trying to predict markets that are influenced mostly by speculation, why not focusing on less speculative markets?"
- "wine companies and consumptions are not influence much by speculations rather than by social trends, economy, age or opening of new markets like cannabis, hard seltzers, psychedelics or other mind altering things like alcohol"
- "try to see if you can forecast those markets based on these factors + the news"

What it needs to be true, in this project's terms:
- **The fundamentals lead the stocks.** Information about alcohol demand that is public today (the economy, what people search for, the price of alcohol, the news) carries information about alcohol companies' stock returns over the next month that their prices don't carry yet.
- **"Less speculative" means more forecastable.** The same kind of information should forecast alcohol stocks better than it forecasts more speculative parts of the market. A failure on alcohol alone doesn't answer that, so the same forecasts are run on SPY, consumer discretionary (XLY), the Nasdaq-100 (QQQ) and consumer staples (XLP) (check C2).
- **What this test can't measure.** Luca asked to forecast "those markets", which includes the wine and alcohol market itself: what people buy. This project measures stock prices. Alcohol sales enter here as an input, and check C4 asks whether the same information forecasts the sales themselves, but the test is about the stocks.

## Published record (novelty and background check, 2026-10-26)

Web searches made while writing this plan, before any data was computed. Only what was read is cited, with where it was read.

**Age (demographics):**
- **DellaVigna & Pollet 2007**, *American Economic Review* 97(5): 1667–1702, "Demographics and Industry Returns" (read: the abstract and introduction of the authors' accepted version, eml.berkeley.edu). Cohort sizes make demand for age-sensitive goods (their examples include beer) forecastable years ahead. Across 48 US industries over 1939–2003, "forecasted demand changes 5 to 10 years in the future predict annual industry stock returns", while "forecasted demand changes over shorter horizons do not predict stock returns." A strategy built on it earned about 6% a year, risk-adjusted. The test is a cross-section of 48 industries at five- to ten-year horizons, with consumption-by-age tables from four expenditure surveys.

**Social trends in search data:**
- **Da, Engelberg & Gao, "In Search of Fundamentals"** (SSRN, 2010, revised 2011; read: the abstract, reproduced on a fund company's page). Rising Google search volume for a firm's most popular products predicts positive revenue surprises, and the signal is strongest in returns around earnings announcements. It is a firm-level, product-level result, not an industry timing rule.
- The project's own tests of Google Trends are below ("What the project already measured").

**Alcohol stocks' returns:**
- **Hong & Kacperczyk 2009**, *Journal of Financial Economics* 93(1): 15–36, "The Price of Sin" (read: NYU Stern's press release and later papers' summaries, not the paper). Alcohol, tobacco and gambling stocks earned about 2.5% a year more than comparable stocks, attributed to norm-constrained institutions avoiding them. A newspaper summary gives a different period and size (1965–2006, about 3.5 points), so the figures are unverified here.
- **Blitz & Fabozzi 2017**, *Journal of Portfolio Management*, "Sin Stocks Revisited" (read: Robeco's summary and the abstract). The sin-stock premium is fully explained by the Fama–French profitability and investment factors.
- These are about the *level* of alcohol stocks' returns, not about forecasting them month to month from demand.

**New markets (cannabis legalization):**
- **Substitution or complement?** A 2024 seminar abstract (Lingnan University, "Antidote for Sin Stock? Evidence from Marijuana Legalization") describes an event study of tobacco and alcohol stocks around state legalizations, testing exactly this; it lists no results. A summary of studies (reported by VinePair and NORML, read there) counts 30 studies finding cannabis a substitute for alcohol and 17 a complement. A scanner-data study found alcohol sales fell about 15% in counties with legal recreational cannabis; an industry-funded study found spirits sales rose.
- **No published event study of alcohol stocks** around legalizations was found.

**What wasn't found:** a published test of monthly industry timing for alcohol stocks from the economy, alcohol prices, search trends and the news. So the bar is the new-idea bar, t ≥ 3.

## Rules (proposed; fixed before any result is seen)

### Timeline and prices

- **The day axis** is Yahoo SPY's trading days. From 2001-12-31 it equals the exports' days (as checked for the credit-spread test).
- **Decision days** are `-3.2`'s calendar, export days 252 + 21k, extended back in steps of 21 trading days to the start of the data. Everything is computed after the close of decision day t₀. The basket is bought at the close of t₀ + 1 and held to the close of t₀ + 22.
- **Prices** are Yahoo's adjusted closes (dividends and splits), so total returns. They are cut in code at the last day of the run's export: 2025-06-27 for the old span, 2026-10-19 for the unseen months.
- **Periods, by decision date:** development 2008-01-01..2019-12-31 (the pass test); check 2020-01-01 to the last decision whose hold ends by 2025-06-27; the unseen months 2025-06-30..2026-10-19, once, at the end.

### The baskets and targets

- **ALC, the primary basket:** STZ, BF-B, TAP, SAM and MGPI, the US-listed alcohol producers whose largest market is the United States. They are equal-weighted at each buy and held without rebalancing for the 21 days. Its return is log(mean over the five of P(t₀ + 22) / P(t₀ + 1)). All five trade from 1995-11-21.
  - **The list is chosen today,** and all five survived. US-listed alcohol producers that left (for example Craft Brew Alliance, acquired 2020; Vintage Wine Estates, bankrupt 2024; Duckhorn, taken private 2024) aren't in the data. That raises the basket's average return, which the intercept absorbs in P1 but which flatters S2's after-costs number.
- **The primary target:** y(t₀) = ALC's 21-day log return minus SPY's over the same days.
- **Reported targets (C2):** ALC alone; ALC − XLP; GLOBAL − SPY, where GLOBAL is DEO, BUD (from 2009-07), CCU and ABEV, equal-weighted the same way; XLP − SPY; XLY − SPY; QQQ − SPY; SPY alone.

### The six inputs (all point in time)

Month m is a calendar month; month(t₀) is the month of the decision date.
- **E1, unemployment:** U(m) − U(m − 12), U = UNRATENSA (not seasonally adjusted, so not revised by seasonal factors).
- **E2, consumer sentiment:** log S(m) − log S(m − 12), S = UMCSENT (the month's final value).
- **P, the relative price of alcohol:** [log A(m) − log A(m − 12)] − [log C(m) − log C(m − 12)], A = CPI alcoholic beverages and C = CPI all items, both NSA. BLS doesn't revise NSA CPI.
- **T1, searches for alcohol:** log G(m) − log G(m − 12), G = the alcohol query.
- **T2, searches for moderation:** the same for the moderation query.
- **T3, searches for new markets:** the same for the new-markets query.

Twelve-month changes remove the strong seasonality of drinking (December, summer, Dry January) and the slow drift in each series. The Google series' scale (100 = the series' own peak) cancels in log changes.

**When each month is public:**
- **E1, E2, P:** month m is used at t₀ only if m ≤ month(t₀) − 2. The Employment Situation and CPI for month m come out in the first three weeks of m + 1, and sentiment's final value at the end of m. So this is about a month later than necessary, for safety.
  - Known late releases (the shutdowns of 2013 and 2025) are applied from their actual release dates, checked against BLS's notices before each run: in 2013, September's data came out on 2013-10-22 (unemployment) and 2013-10-30 (CPI), both before the first November decision that uses them; in 2025, September's unemployment came out on 2025-11-20, so a decision before that date uses August.
  - A month with no value (2025-10) is skipped: the latest earlier month whose 12-month change can be computed is used.
- **T1–T3:** month m is used if m ≤ month(t₀) − 1 and t₀ is on or after the 8th of its month; otherwise m ≤ month(t₀) − 2. Google posts a complete month within days.
- **Google's data is one download** (2026-10-26). Google Trends samples searches, so a later download differs slightly, and its scale is set by a peak that may lie after t₀. Neither changes a log change by more than rounding, but the values weren't recorded at t₀.

### P1 (primary). Do the six inputs, combined, forecast ALC against SPY?

- **Baseline:** the prevailing mean, the average of y over the eligible decisions.
- **The combination forecast:** each input k gets its own least-squares fit with an intercept, y = a_k + b_k · x_k, on the eligible decisions, and forecasts y(t₀) from x_k(t₀). The combination is the average of the six forecasts (the equal-weight combination of Rapach, Strauss & Zhou 2010, *Review of Financial Studies* 23(2), not re-read for this plan). Averaging six small forecasts is far less prone to overfitting than one regression on six inputs, which is reported beside it (C1).
- **Eligibility:** a fit at t₀ may use decision s only if s's target has ended: s + 22 ≤ t₀ on the day axis (asserted for every fit). With decisions 21 days apart, that is two decisions back.
- **Sample:** the decisions where all six inputs and y exist (from about 2005-03, when Google's 12-month changes begin). A fit needs at least 36 eligible decisions, so forecasts start in about 2008-05. Every model, the baseline included, is fitted on the same decisions.
- **Clark-West:** f = e₀² − e_c² + (ŷ₀ − ŷ_c)², where e is a model's error and ŷ its forecast, 0 the baseline and c the combination. Its mean's t-statistic uses Newey-West standard errors with 3 lags (the holds don't overlap, but the inputs are persistent). The bootstrap is a moving-block bootstrap of the centred f with 12-decision blocks and 10,000 draws; the one-sided p is the share of resampled means at or above the observed mean.
- **Pass (development):** all three of
  1. Clark-West t ≥ 3;
  2. bootstrap p < 0.0013;
  3. a positive out-of-sample R² against the baseline: 1 − MSE_c ÷ MSE₀ > 0.
- **Check (2020–2025-06):** consistent if the out-of-sample R² is positive and Clark-West's one-sided normal p is below 0.05.
- **Reported with P1:** the out-of-sample R² with a 95% block-bootstrap interval; by year; without 2008–2009 and without 2020; each input's fitted slope over time.
- **No sign is required.** Luca didn't say which way each input should push.

### S2 (secondary). A basket-or-SPY rule

- **The rule:** at each decision, hold ALC for the next 21 days if the combination forecasts y > 0, otherwise SPY.
- **Costs:** 5 bps on every traded dollar: the first buy, both legs of a switch, and the trades that return ALC to equal weights at each buy.
- **Bar (development decisions):** after costs, the rule's mean monthly log return beats both always-ALC and always-SPY, each with a paired 12-month block-bootstrap p < 0.10 (10,000 draws). It counts only if P1 passes.
- **Reported:** each account's annual return, Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown, share of months in ALC and switches; by period (2008–2009, 2010–2014, 2015–2019, 2020–2025-06); and the variant that holds ALC when the combination is above the prevailing mean, which isolates the inputs' timing from the basket's average premium.

**The README numbers come from S2's rule:**
- **Before costs, vs random:** its compound annual return at 0 bps minus the median of 10,000 random reorderings of its own monthly ALC-or-SPY choices, decisions 2008-01..2025-06 (from the first forecast); random p beside it; by period.
- **After costs:** its yearly return at 5 bps a side minus SPY's total return (Yahoo), over calendar years 2008..2025 by trading day (2008 from the first trade's close; 2025 to the last hold's end), averaged. Always-ALC's is reported beside it.

### Checks (reported, not pass/fail)

- **C1. Each input alone,** against the prevailing mean: Clark-West t and out-of-sample R², development and check, with Holm-adjusted p across the six for information. Also one regression on all six (the "kitchen sink").
- **C2. Is "less speculative" more forecastable?** The P1 pipeline, unchanged, on each reported target: ALC alone, ALC − XLP, GLOBAL − SPY, XLP − SPY, XLY − SPY, QQQ − SPY and SPY alone. Twice: with the six inputs, and with the economy pair E1 and E2 only (the inputs that aren't about alcohol).
  - Luca's premise predicts the alcohol targets come out more forecastable than XLY, QQQ and SPY.
  - If the alcohol-specific inputs forecast XLY − SPY or QQQ − SPY as well as ALC − SPY, any success isn't about alcohol.
  - Beside it, how "speculative" each target is: the annualized volatility of its 21-day returns, its beta and correlation to SPY, and the share of its variance SPY explains.
- **C3. Retail sales (revised data, a look-ahead):** R = the 12-month log change in beer, wine and liquor stores' share of all retail sales (MRTSSM4453USS ÷ MRTSSM44X72USS), month m used if m ≤ month(t₀) − 3 (the store-level detail comes out about six weeks after the month). As a seventh input in the combination, and alone. The values are FRED's final vintage, so revisions after t₀ are in them.
- **C4. Are the sales themselves forecastable?** On calendar months: forecast the 12-month growth of beer, wine and liquor store sales three months ahead from the information of month m. Out-of-sample R² against the expanding mean, for (a) the latest growth alone (an AR(1) in the growth rate) and (b) the six inputs' combination plus the latest growth. 2008–2019 and 2020–2025. This is the "consumption" half of Luca's question. Revised data, so reported only.
- **C5. New markets: cannabis legalization events.** The list is fixed here. Each date is the day the decision became public; for an election, results arrive after the close, so the first reaction is the next day's close.
  - Ballot measures (election day): 2012-11-06 (Colorado, Washington); 2014-11-04 (Alaska, Oregon, DC); 2016-11-08 (California, Nevada, Massachusetts, Maine); 2018-11-06 (Michigan); 2020-11-03 (Arizona, Montana, New Jersey, South Dakota; Oregon's psilocybin measure); 2022-11-08 (Maryland, Missouri; Colorado's psychedelics measure); 2023-11-07 (Ohio).
  - Laws (signing or final passage): 2018-01-22 (Vermont); 2018-06-19 (Canada's Senate passes C-45); 2019-06-25 (Illinois); 2021-03-31 (New York); 2021-04-07 (Virginia); 2021-04-12 (New Mexico); 2021-06-22 (Connecticut); 2022-05-25 (Rhode Island); 2023-04-24 (Delaware, law without signature on Sunday the 23rd); 2023-05-30 (Minnesota).
  - Federal: 2023-08-30 (HHS recommends moving cannabis to Schedule III, reported that afternoon); 2024-04-30 (reports that the DEA will reschedule).
  - **Measured:** ALC − SPY log returns cumulated over days +1..+2 and +1..+21 after the event day's close, and over the 20 days before (anticipation). Mean across events, a t-statistic across events, and the share negative. Under substitution, alcohol loses. The cannabis basket (TLRY, CGC, ACB, CRON, SNDL, whichever trade that day) against SPY for events from 2018-06 on, where substitution says cannabis gains.
  - **Its limits:** about 19 dates, 7 of them in 2008–2019; election outcomes are partly known from polls, and a signing follows a public vote, so much of the news is priced before the date. The psychedelics measures share their dates with cannabis measures and can't be separated.
- **C6. The news.** Headlines from the catalog's Reuters US archive (2007–2014, parsed with its `parse_page`) and HuffPost's dataset (2012-01..2022-09).
  - **An alcohol headline** matches (case-insensitive, whole words) alcohol, alcoholic, wine, wines, winery, wineries, vineyard, vineyards, beer, beers, brewer, brewers, brewery, breweries, brewing, liquor, whiskey, whisky, vodka, tequila, bourbon, cocktail, cocktails, seltzer, Budweiser, Anheuser, Molson, Coors, Diageo, Heineken, "Constellation Brands", "Brown-Forman", "Boston Beer", or "Dry January".
  - **Monthly series per source:** the share of headlines that are alcohol headlines, and their mean VADER compound score (the catalog's scorer). Each is standardized by its own previous 36 months (point in time) and averaged over the sources that have that month.
  - **Inputs:** N1 (the share) and N2 (the tone), month m used if m ≤ month(t₀) − 1, each alone and added to the six, on the months they cover. The sources stop in 2022-09 and change mix in 2012, so this is reported only.
- **C7. Extra caution:** every input one month older.
- **C8. A wider basket:** ALC plus GLOBAL (nine names) against SPY.
- **C9. Code checks, run before P1's result is read:**
  - **noise:** 200 sets of six independent normal series and 200 sets of six AR(1) series with coefficient 0.95 (as persistent as 12-month changes) go through the whole P1 pipeline in place of the inputs. They show how often noise reaches t ≥ 3 and a positive R²;
  - **a leaky oracle:** the future y plus noise, which must win by far.
- **C10. Age is not tested.** DellaVigna & Pollet's evidence is a cross-section of 48 industries at five- to ten-year horizons, built from consumption-by-age surveys. One industry's monthly returns can't test it: the demographic forecast changes once a year and moves like a time trend. Their own finding is that short-horizon demand forecasts don't predict returns.

### What would count

- **P1 passes:** the fundamentals forecast alcohol stocks against the market, and S2 decides whether it's worth money.
- **P1 fails and C2 shows the alcohol targets no more forecastable than XLY, QQQ or SPY:** being "less speculative" doesn't make a stock more forecastable from public fundamentals.
- **C4 shows sales forecastable and P1 fails:** the demand is forecastable, but the stock price already reflects it.

## Decisions (2026-10-26, during and after the run)

1. **Before P1 was read: the oracle's strength in C9.** The plan says only that the leaky oracle "must win by far". The code first asserted t > 5. The oracle with noise as large as the target won clearly (R² +0.69) but reached t 4.64 and bootstrap p 0.0014, just above P1's 0.0013. The assertion was relaxed to t > 3 and R² > 0.3, and oracles with noise 0.5, 2 and 4 times the target's size were added to show the bar's reach. The 4× oracle (R² +0.13) passed the whole bar (t 4.79, p 0.0001), so the bar can be met, but only by a forecast far stronger than any published monthly predictor. P1's bar was not changed.
2. **After the first run of C5: the event day's own return,** added as a reported column. News that broke during trading (the DEA report of 2024-04-30) moved prices on day e itself, which the plan's windows count in the 20 days before. The plan's windows are unchanged.
3. **The decision of 2025-06-13 is in neither span.** Its hold ends after the old export's last day, and its date is before the unseen months begin. It is left out.
4. **The check period's bootstrap p** (65 decisions, about five 12-decision blocks) is unreliable, and the plan judges the check by the normal p. One case disagrees sharply: C7's check bootstrap p is 0.0009 against a normal p of 0.036. It isn't used.
5. **The news keywords catch some false matches** ("Milwaukee Brewers", "polyvinyl alcohol"). The list was fixed in the plan and wasn't changed. HuffPost's alcohol headlines thin out after 2018 (fewer than five a month), so the tone input N2 covers 2010-02..2018-05 only.
6. **Late releases, checked 2026-10-26:** BLS released September 2025's Employment Situation on 2025-11-20 (its archive, `empsit_11202025.pdf`) and collected no household survey for October 2025. The decision of 2025-11-12 used August's unemployment, and the decision of 2025-12-12 used September's unemployment and CPI because October's don't exist (`inputs_new.txt`). The 2013 dates needed no change: September 2013's data was first used in November.
7. **What the code imports read-only:** `research/lib/c2eval.py` (the exports' days), the catalog's `gtfetch.py` (Google Trends download and parser), `build_reuters.py` (the Reuters page parser and capture rule) and `score.py` (VADER). Nothing in the repo or the catalog was edited.
